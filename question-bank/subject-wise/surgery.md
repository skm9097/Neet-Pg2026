### Q1 — DIEP flap identification

Which is true about the flap shown in the image?

- A. Pedicled TRAM based on superior epigastric artery
- B. Free TRAM based on superior and inferior epigastric artery
- C. DIEP based on deep inferior epigastric artery
- D. Lower abdominal fat based on inferior epigastric vessels

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. DIEP (deep inferior epigastric perforator) flap** — Free flap using skin and fat only (no muscle), based on perforators of deep inferior epigastric artery. Preserves rectus abdominis → less donor site morbidity than TRAM.
</details>

### Q2 — MEN 1 gastrinoma location

In MEN 1 syndrome, where does gastrinoma most frequently occur?

- A. Jejunum
- B. Ileum
- C. Duodenum
- D. Stomach

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Duodenum** — Gastrinoma triangle: between cystic duct, junction of 2nd/3rd duodenum, and pancreatic neck/body junction. Duodenal gastrinomas are most common (70%); smaller and more often multiple in MEN 1.
</details>

### Q3 — Aortoiliac occlusion (Leriche syndrome)

50-year-old male smoker with bilateral thigh and buttock claudication after ~500 m. Diagnosis?

- A. Superficial femoral artery disease
- B. Aortoiliac arterial disease (Leriche syndrome)
- C. Femoral venous insufficiency
- D. Profunda femoris disease

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. Aortoiliac arterial disease (Leriche syndrome)** — Triad: bilateral buttock/thigh claudication, impotence, absent femoral pulses. Caused by aortoiliac occlusive disease. Managed by aortobifemoral bypass or endovascular stenting.
</details>

### Q4 — Retrosternal goiter: true statement

Which statement regarding retrosternal goiter is accurate?

- A. Operated in all patients regardless of symptoms
- B. Sternal incision is always required
- C. Receives blood supply from the thoracodorsal artery
- D. Most can be removed by neck incision

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Most retrosternal goiters can be removed by neck incision** — ~95% can be delivered through a cervical (collar) incision because blood supply comes from the neck. Sternotomy only needed for true intrathoracic goiters with independent thoracic blood supply.
</details>

### Q5 — Hinchey Stage 4 diverticular disease

Diverticular perforation with fecal peritonitis at surgery = which Hinchey stage?

- A. Stage 1 (pericolic abscess)
- B. Stage 2 (pelvic/distant abscess)
- C. Stage 3 (purulent peritonitis)
- D. Stage 4 (fecal peritonitis)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Stage 4** — Hinchey classification: Stage 1 = pericolic abscess; Stage 2 = distant abscess; Stage 3 = purulent peritonitis; Stage 4 = fecal peritonitis. Stage 4 needs Hartmann's procedure or primary resection.
</details>

### Q6 — Low-risk prostate cancer management

75-year-old with prostate carcinoma, PSA 9 ng/mL, small tumour focus, Gleason score 6. Management?

- A. Radical prostatectomy
- B. External beam radiation
- C. Brachytherapy
- D. Active surveillance

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Active surveillance** — Low-risk prostate cancer (PSA < 10, Gleason ≤ 6, T1-T2a) in elderly patients: active surveillance with PSA monitoring and serial biopsies is recommended to avoid overtreatment.
</details>

### Q7 — Paediatric abdominal trauma: stable child

Child with RTA, stable vitals, left lumbar tenderness. Best investigation?

- A. Contrast-enhanced CT scan
- B. Retrograde urethrogram
- C. Wait and watch
- D. Emergency laparotomy

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A. Contrast-enhanced CT scan** — Haemodynamically stable child with blunt abdominal trauma: CECT abdomen is gold standard for grading solid organ injuries (liver, spleen, kidney). FAST is useful for unstable patients.
</details>

### Q8 — Thiersch wiring for rectal prolapse

Postoperative image shows which procedure for rectal prolapse?

- A. Stapled haemorrhoidopexy
- B. Well's procedure
- C. Thiersch wiring
- D. Altemeier repair

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Thiersch wiring** — Encircles anal canal with a wire/suture to narrow it; simple perineal procedure for frail elderly patients with complete rectal prolapse. High recurrence rate; used when abdominal procedures are too risky.
</details>

### Q9 — Urethral injury: blood at meatus, stable patient

Motorcycle RTA with blood at urethral meatus, haemodynamically stable. Next management?

- A. Foley catheterisation
- B. Suprapubic cystostomy
- C. Retrograde urethrogram
- D. Wait and watch

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Retrograde urethrogram (RUG)** — Blood at urethral meatus = urethral injury until proved otherwise. Do NOT insert catheter (can convert partial to complete tear). First do RUG to characterise injury, then SPC if needed.
</details>

### Q10 — Direct inguinal hernia and repair

Hernia medial to inferior epigastric artery. Diagnosis and management?

- A. Direct hernia — Bassini repair
- B. Direct hernia — Lichtenstein mesh repair
- C. Indirect hernia — Bassini repair
- D. Indirect hernia — Lichtenstein mesh repair

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. Direct hernia — Lichtenstein mesh repair** — Medial to inferior epigastric artery = direct hernia (Hesselbach's triangle). Lichtenstein tension-free mesh repair is current gold standard for inguinal hernias.
</details>

### Q11 — Vesicoureteric reflux in child with recurrent UTIs

6-year-old with recurrent UTIs. Imaging shows which diagnosis?

- A. Vesicoureteric reflux
- B. Urinary bladder diverticulum
- C. Urinary bladder hernia
- D. Vesicocolic fistula

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A. Vesicoureteric reflux (VUR)** — Most common cause of recurrent UTIs in children; diagnosed by MCU (micturating cystourethrogram). Graded I–V; high grades associated with renal scarring.
</details>

### Q12 — Post-thyroidectomy perioral numbness: investigation

45-year-old female, perioral numbness 3 days post-thyroidectomy. Required investigations?

- A. Free T3, T4
- B. T3, T4, TSH
- C. Radioiodine scan
- D. Calcium, phosphate, and PTH levels

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Calcium, phosphate, and PTH** — Perioral numbness/tingling after thyroidectomy = hypoparathyroidism (accidental parathyroid removal/ischaemia) → hypocalcaemia → Chvostek's/Trousseau's signs. Check serum calcium urgently.
</details>

### Q13 — Thromboelastography

The coagulation monitoring method shown (graphical waveform of clot formation) is:

- A. Thromboelastography (TEG)
- B. Plethysmography
- C. Sonography
- D. Elastography

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A. Thromboelastography (TEG)** — Measures viscoelastic properties of whole blood clot formation; provides global picture of coagulation (clot initiation, amplification, stabilisation, fibrinolysis); guides intraoperative transfusion.
</details>

### Q14 — Peau d'orange in breast cancer: cause

59-year-old with painless breast lump and "peau d'orange" skin change. Cause?

- A. Infiltration of subdermal lymphatics
- B. Infiltration of lactiferous duct
- C. Involvement of Cooper's ligament
- D. Spread to anterior chest wall

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A. Infiltration of subdermal lymphatics** — Tumour emboli in subdermal lymphatics cause lymphoedema of skin; sweat gland orifices remain tethered → orange-peel appearance. Cooper's ligament involvement = skin dimpling/retraction.
</details>

### Q16 — Osteoblastic bone secondaries

Predominantly osteoblastic metastases occur in:

- A. Prostate carcinoma
- B. Breast carcinoma
- C. Stomach carcinoma
- D. Bone carcinoma

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A. Prostate carcinoma** — Prostate metastases are classically osteoblastic (sclerotic) on X-ray; elevated ALP and PSA. Breast can be mixed. Lung, kidney, thyroid, colon = lytic. Mnemonic: "Pb" (prostate/breast) blastic.
</details>

### Q17 — Cyclophosphamide and bladder carcinoma

Which drug causes bladder carcinoma?

- A. Cyclophosphamide
- B. Cisplatin
- C. Taxane
- D. Tamoxifen

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A. Cyclophosphamide** — Acrolein (toxic metabolite) causes haemorrhagic cystitis and increases risk of transitional cell carcinoma of bladder. Prevented with mesna (uroprotection). Tamoxifen → endometrial carcinoma.
</details>

### Q18 — Branchial cyst location

Most common site of a branchial cyst?

- A. Junction of upper and middle third of sternomastoid
- B. Junction of middle and lower third of sternomastoid
- C. Middle of sternomastoid
- D. Between two heads of sternomastoid

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A. Junction of upper and middle third of sternomastoid** — Second branchial cleft cyst (most common type); presents as fluctuant swelling at upper anterior triangle, deep to sternomastoid, lateral to carotid sheath.
</details>

### Q19 — Dohlman's procedure

Dohlman's procedure is performed for:

- A. Meckel's diverticulum
- B. Zenker's diverticulum
- C. Bochdalek hernia
- D. Menetrier's disease

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. Zenker's diverticulum** — Endoscopic Dohlman's procedure: division of the common wall (cricopharyngeal bar) between the diverticulum and oesophagus using rigid endoscopy. Minimally invasive alternative to open surgery.
</details>

### Q20 — Parathyroid autotransplantation site

Parathyroid gland is commonly autotransplanted to:

- A. Triceps
- B. Biceps
- C. Sartorius
- D. Brachioradialis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Brachioradialis** — Non-dominant forearm (brachioradialis muscle) is the standard autotransplantation site; allows easy monitoring of PTH and re-exploration if needed under local anaesthesia.
</details>
