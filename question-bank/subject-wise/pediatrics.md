### Q1 — Meningococcal meningitis exposure: management

Two classmates diagnosed with meningococcal meningitis. What is the correct guidance for exposed students?

- A. Two doses of polysaccharide vaccine
- B. Antibiotic prophylaxis
- C. Two doses of conjugate vaccine
- D. Single dose of meningococcal vaccine

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. Antibiotic prophylaxis** — Close contacts of N. meningitidis: rifampicin (first-line) × 2 days; ciprofloxacin single dose; or ceftriaxone IM single dose. Chemoprophylaxis must be started within 24–48 hours of exposure. Vaccine is adjunct, not replacement.
</details>

### Q2 — Kwashiorkor diagnosis

Child with enlarged abdomen, poor facial appearance, oedema, diet of rice water, low serum albumin. Diagnosis?

- A. Kwashiorkor
- B. Marasmus
- C. Indian childhood cirrhosis
- D. Kawasaki disease

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A. Kwashiorkor** — Protein deficiency (adequate calories but protein-poor diet); oedema, hypoalbuminaemia, "flaky-paint" skin, flag sign in hair, fatty liver, moon-face. Marasmus = severe calorie deficiency, no oedema, "old man face."
</details>

### Q3 — Koplik spots and measles

3-year-old male with Koplik spots on buccal mucosa, develops rash after 3 days. Diagnosis?

- A. Measles
- B. Leukoplakia
- C. Scarlet fever
- D. Kawasaki disease

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A. Measles** — Koplik spots (white spots on red background at lower molar level) are pathognomonic prodromal sign; appear 1–2 days before rash; rash is maculopapular, starts behind ears → spreads centrifugally. Rubeola virus (Paramyxovirus).
</details>

### Q4 — Newborn's first stool: colour cause

Greenish-black colour of meconium in the first stool is caused by:

- A. Bilirubin
- B. Biliverdin
- C. Bile pigments, swallowed amniotic fluid debris, and intestinal secretions (meconium)
- D. Gut flora of neonate

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Meconium** — Meconium contains swallowed amniotic fluid, bile, lanugo, intestinal cells, and digestive secretions; dark green-black due to bile pigments (biliverdin is a component). Passed within first 24–48 hours; failure suggests Hirschsprung's disease.
</details>

### Q5 — Diphtheria contact management (vaccinated sibling)

3-year-old vaccinated sibling of a diphtheria case, living in the same household. Management?

- A. One booster dose
- B. Nothing needed (already exposed)
- C. Erythromycin + diphtheria toxoid
- D. Erythromycin only

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Erythromycin only** — For household contacts of diphtheria: erythromycin × 7–10 days (chemoprophylaxis) regardless of vaccination status; booster dose if immunisation > 5 years ago. Fully vaccinated recent contacts: erythromycin alone is sufficient.
</details>

### Q6 — Henoch-Schönlein purpura

Child with palpable non-blanching purpura (buttocks/lower limbs), abdominal pain, joint pain (knees), haematuria, proteinuria. Diagnosis?

- A. Immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP)
- B. Dengue
- C. Churg-Strauss syndrome
- D. Henoch-Schönlein purpura (IgA vasculitis)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Henoch-Schönlein purpura** — Most common systemic vasculitis in children; IgA deposition in small vessel walls; tetrad: palpable purpura + arthritis + abdominal pain + renal involvement. Platelet count normal (distinguishes from ITP).
</details>

### Q7 — Pyloric stenosis metabolic abnormality

Infant with projectile non-bilious vomiting and olive-shaped epigastric mass. Metabolic abnormality?

- A. Hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic acidosis
- B. Hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic alkalosis
- C. Hypokalaemic hyperchloraemic alkalosis
- D. Hyperchloraemic hypokalaemic acidosis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. Hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic alkalosis** — Loss of HCl in vomitus → metabolic alkalosis + hypochloraemia; kidneys try to conserve Na but exchange K and H → hypokalaemia, paradoxical aciduria. Classic for hypertrophic pyloric stenosis (3–6 weeks of age).
</details>

### Q8 — Hypophosphataemic rickets

6-year-old girl with rachitic changes since age 2, multiple Vit D courses failed, normal parents. Lab: Ca 9.5, PO₄ 1.6, ALP 814, PTH normal, normal electrolytes, creatinine, and ABG. Diagnosis?

- A. Vitamin D-dependent rickets
- B. Hypoparathyroidism
- C. Chronic renal failure
- D. Hypophosphataemic rickets (X-linked)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Hypophosphataemic rickets (X-linked)** — PHEX gene mutation → elevated FGF-23 → impaired renal phosphate reabsorption → low phosphate → rickets resistant to Vit D. Normal Ca, normal PTH, isolated low phosphate. Treated with phosphate supplements + calcitriol.
</details>

### Q9 — Height-for-age < -2 SD: interpretation

Child's height-for-age Z-score is < -2 SD. This indicates?

- A. Chronic malnutrition (stunting)
- B. Acute malnutrition (wasting)
- C. Recent infection
- D. No malnutrition

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A. Chronic malnutrition (stunting)** — Height-for-age < -2 SD = stunting (chronic long-standing malnutrition/recurrent infections). Weight-for-height < -2 SD = wasting (acute malnutrition). Weight-for-age < -2 SD = underweight.
</details>

### Q10 — Nephrotic syndrome

Child with generalised swelling, hypercholesterolaemia, 3+ proteinuria, lipiduria (oval fat bodies). Diagnosis?

- A. Nephrotic syndrome
- B. Nephritic syndrome
- C. Goodpasture's disease
- D. IgA nephropathy

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A. Nephrotic syndrome** — Tetrad: massive proteinuria (> 3.5 g/day adults; > 40 mg/m²/hr children) + hypoalbuminaemia + oedema + hyperlipidaemia. Oval fat bodies (lipid-laden tubular cells) in urine. Most common cause in children: minimal change disease.
</details>

### Q11 — 1-day-old neonate who has not passed urine: management

1-day-old neonate has not passed urine since birth. Next step?

- A. Continue breastfeeding and observe
- B. Admit to NICU
- C. Start artificial feeding
- D. Start intravenous fluids

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A. Continue breastfeeding and observe** — Normal neonates may not void for up to 24–48 hours; reassure and continue breastfeeding. If no urine by 48 hours → investigate for renal agenesis, posterior urethral valves, or urethral obstruction.
</details>

### Q12 — Tumour lysis syndrome: management priority

7-year-old post-chemotherapy with hyperuricaemia, elevated creatinine, hyperkalaemia (TLS). First priority?

- A. Aggressive IV hydration
- B. Probenecid
- C. Allopurinol
- D. Rasburicase

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A. Aggressive IV hydration** — First step in TLS management: vigorous IV hydration to maintain urine output ≥ 2 mL/kg/hr → promotes uric acid excretion + prevents renal deposition. Rasburicase for elevated uric acid; allopurinol for prophylaxis.
</details>

### Q13 — Intussusception: coiled spring sign

Baby with colicky pain and a mass in the right iliac fossa; barium enema shows coiled spring sign. Diagnosis?

- A. Intussusception
- B. Volvulus
- C. Duodenal atresia
- D. Intestinal obstruction (other)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A. Intussusception** — Telescoping of bowel (usually ileocolic); peak age 3 months–3 years; currant jelly stools, sausage-shaped mass; coiled spring sign on barium/contrast enema (also diagnostic/therapeutic). First-line: air/hydrostatic reduction enema.
</details>

### Q14 — Neonatal hypothyroidism: TSH > 100 mIU/L investigation

8-day-old newborn, TSH > 100 mIU/L on heel-prick screen. Most appropriate next test?

- A. Urine iodine excretion
- B. Serum thyroid receptor antibody
- C. Radiotracer uptake with technetium
- D. Perchlorate secretion test

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Radiotracer uptake with technetium-99m** — To determine the cause of congenital hypothyroidism: thyroid scan identifies thyroid aplasia, hypoplasia, ectopic thyroid, or dyshormonogenesis. Start L-thyroxine immediately without waiting for scan results.
</details>
