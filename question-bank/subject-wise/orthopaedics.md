## Joint Disease

### Q1 — Osteoarthritis distribution (PIP, DIP, CMC sparing wrist/MCP)

PIP, DIP and 1st CMC involvement with sparing of wrist/MCP joints. Diagnosis?

- A. Rheumatoid arthritis
- B. Psoriatic arthritis
- C. Pseudogout
- D. Osteoarthritis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Osteoarthritis** — Classic OA distribution: DIP (Heberden's nodes), PIP (Bouchard's nodes), 1st CMC (thumb base squaring). RA = MCP, PIP, wrists.
</details>

---

### Q2 — Heberden's nodes location

Heberden's nodes are seen at:

- A. Hip
- B. Knee
- C. MCP
- D. DIP

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. DIP joints** — Distal interphalangeal joint bony swellings of osteoarthritis. Bouchard's nodes = PIP joints. Both are bony, NOT inflammatory.
</details>

---

### Q3 — RA: symmetric small joint with anti-CCP positive

45-year-old woman with symmetric PIP/MCP/wrist involvement and elevated RF + anti-CCP. Diagnosis?

- A. Osteoarthritis
- B. Rheumatoid arthritis
- C. Gout
- D. Psoriatic arthritis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. Rheumatoid arthritis** — Anti-CCP is highly specific (~95%) and predicts erosive disease. Classic distribution: symmetric MCPs, PIPs, wrists, MTPs.
</details>

---

### Q4 — Joint typically spared in osteoarthritis

Which joint is characteristically SPARED in OA?

- A. Hip
- B. Knee
- C. MCP
- D. Spine

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. MCP joints** — Primary OA spares MCPs (and wrists, ankles). MCP involvement suggests RA, haemochromatosis, or psoriatic arthritis. OA classically affects DIPs, PIPs, CMC, hip, knee, spine, 1st MTP.
</details>

### Q5 — Ankylosing spondylitis (young male, morning stiffness, heel pain)

20-year-old male with 2-year lower backache, morning stiffness, bilateral heel pain. Most likely diagnosis?

- A. Tuberculosis of spine
- B. Disc prolapse
- C. Mechanical back pain
- D. Ankylosing spondylitis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Ankylosing spondylitis** — Inflammatory back pain (improves with activity), morning stiffness, enthesitis (heel pain), HLA-B27 association, anterior uveitis. Bamboo spine on later X-ray.
</details>

## Paediatric Orthopaedics

### Q6 — Pulled elbow (nursemaid's elbow)

A maid spins a child by hands; child later refuses to use the arm. Diagnosis?

- A. Olecranon fracture
- B. Fracture head of radius
- C. Elbow dislocation
- D. Pulled elbow (radial head subluxation)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Pulled elbow** — Radial head subluxation from annular ligament; common in toddlers (1–4 years) with axial traction. Easy reduction: supination + flexion (or hyperpronation).
</details>

---

### Q7 — Late complication of supracondylar humerus fracture

Most common late complication of supracondylar humerus fracture if left untreated?

- A. Pulled elbow
- B. Dinner-fork deformity
- C. Osteoarthritis
- D. Cubitus varus (gunstock deformity)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Cubitus varus** — From malunion of supracondylar fracture. Acute complication: Volkmann's ischaemic contracture (brachial artery injury). Dinner-fork = Colles' fracture.
</details>

---

### Q8 — Scurvy in 5-year-old with bilateral knee pain

5-year-old with bilateral knee pain, normal BMD, X-ray showing metaphyseal changes (Pelkan spurs, Wimberger ring). Diagnosis?

- A. Rickets
- B. Metaphyseal dysplasia
- C. Pycnodysostosis
- D. Scurvy

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Scurvy** — Vitamin C deficiency in child: ground-glass osteopenia, Wimberger ring (sclerotic epiphyseal ring), Frenkel line (white line at metaphysis), Trummerfeld zone, Pelkan spurs.
</details>

---

### Q9 — Osteogenesis imperfecta on prenatal scan

13-week IUS shows fetus with multiple long bone fractures. Likely diagnosis?

- A. Achondroplasia
- B. Cretinism
- C. Marfan syndrome
- D. Osteogenesis imperfecta

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Osteogenesis imperfecta** — Type I collagen mutation; multiple fractures (even in utero), blue sclera, hearing loss, ligamentous laxity. Type II is the most severe (lethal perinatal form).
</details>

### Q10 — SCFE classification — NOT used

Which is NOT a classification for slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE)?

- A. Southwick (angle)
- B. Loder (stable/unstable)
- C. Salter-Harris
- D. Pacenti

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Salter-Harris** — Used for paediatric growth plate fractures generally, not specifically SCFE. SCFE-specific: Southwick (severity by angle), Loder (stable/unstable), Pinned in situ vs reduction approaches.
</details>

## Trauma & Fractures

### Q11 — Garden's Stage 4 femoral neck

Garden's Stage 4 femoral neck fracture features:

- A. Displacement from acetabulum
- B. Partial alignment with intact trabeculae
- C. Angulation/rotation with disrupted trabeculae
- D. Trabecular alignment of acetabulum and femoral head but not the neck

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Trabecular alignment of acetabulum and femoral head but not the neck** — Garden's IV: completely displaced fracture but realigned trabeculae (rotational realignment). High risk of avascular necrosis → hemiarthroplasty/THR.
</details>

---

### Q12 — Posterior hip dislocation

RTA victim with flexed, adducted, internally rotated, shortened left limb. Diagnosis?

- A. Anterior hip dislocation
- B. Transcervical fracture
- C. Intertrochanteric fracture
- D. Posterior hip dislocation

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Posterior hip dislocation** — Classic dashboard injury. Anterior dislocation: hip abducted, externally rotated, slightly flexed. Risk of sciatic nerve injury and AVN of femoral head.
</details>

### Q13 — Compartment syndrome (tibia fracture)

Closed tibial fracture, severe disproportionate pain, pain on passive dorsiflexion, loss of sensation in 1st web space. Next step?

- A. Elevate and observe
- B. Immediate fasciotomy
- C. Opioid analgesics
- D. Apply cast

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. Immediate fasciotomy** — Compartment syndrome: 5 Ps (Pain, Pallor, Pulselessness — late, Paraesthesia, Paralysis). Compartment pressure > 30 mmHg or Δ < 30 mmHg → fasciotomy urgently.
</details>

---

### Q14 — Volkmann's ischaemic contracture

10-year-old with forearm tense, tender, decreased sensation, marked limitation of wrist/finger extension after supracondylar fracture. Diagnosis?

- A. Radial nerve injury
- B. Volkmann's ischaemic contracture
- C. Compartment syndrome (acute)
- D. Flexor tendon injury

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. Volkmann's ischaemic contracture** — Sequelae of forearm compartment syndrome (brachial artery injury); fibrosis of deep flexors → claw hand. Preventable if early fasciotomy done.
</details>

---

### Q15 — Fat embolism syndrome (femur + tibia fracture)

2 days post-tibia fracture: breathlessness, petechiae, low SpO2. Diagnosis?

- A. Air embolism
- B. VTE
- C. Pulmonary hypertension
- D. Fat embolism

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Fat embolism syndrome** — Classic triad: respiratory + neurological + petechial rash; onset 24–72 hours after long bone fracture. ARDS-like picture on CXR. Treatment: supportive (O2, mechanical ventilation).
</details>

---

### Q16 — Fat embolism (femur fracture, 48 hours, ICU)

48 hours post-femur fracture in ICU: clear lung fields on CXR, but SpO2 60% on 100% O2, confusion. Diagnosis?

- A. Fat embolism
- B. Pulmonary embolism
- C. ARDS
- D. Occult pneumothorax

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A. Fat embolism syndrome** — Refractory hypoxia + altered mental status with relatively clear initial CXR is classic. Diffuse ground-glass on CT later. Petechial rash usually appears.
</details>

### Q17 — Pelvic injury: posterior hip dislocation

40-year-old after RTA with pelvic X-ray showing femoral head superior to acetabulum. Diagnosis?

- A. Central hip dislocation
- B. Fracture shaft of femur
- C. Anterior hip dislocation
- D. Posterior hip dislocation

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Posterior hip dislocation** — Femoral head displaced posterosuperior to acetabulum on AP pelvis X-ray. Most common direction of dislocation.
</details>

### Q18 — Proximal scaphoid: highest non-union risk

Which fracture is most prone to non-union?

- A. Intertrochanteric
- B. Distal radius
- C. Talar neck
- D. Proximal scaphoid

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Proximal scaphoid** — Retrograde blood supply from distal end → proximal pole avascular → high non-union/AVN risk. Talar neck is also notorious for AVN.
</details>

### Q19 — Hill-Sachs lesion

Posterolateral humeral head indentation with anterior shoulder instability. Lesion?

- A. Bankart lesion
- B. Hill-Sachs lesion
- C. SLAP tear
- D. Scapholunate dissociation

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. Hill-Sachs lesion** — Compression fracture of posterolateral humeral head from impact with anterior glenoid during dislocation. Bankart = anterior labroligamentous avulsion from glenoid.
</details>

### Q20 — Chance fracture

Flexion-distraction injury with horizontal fracture through vertebra (anterior wedging + posterior element distraction). Diagnosis?

- A. Burst fracture
- B. Compression fracture
- C. Chance fracture
- D. Impacted fracture

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Chance fracture** — Lap-belt injury; horizontal split through vertebral body and posterior elements; often associated with intra-abdominal injury (hollow viscus injury).
</details>

### Q21 — De-gloving injury definition

De-gloving injury refers to:

- A. Skin alone stripped off
- B. Skin, subcut fat and fascia stripped from tendons
- C. Skin, subcut fat, fascia, and tendons stripped from bone
- D. Skin and subcut fat stripped from underlying fascia

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Skin and subcutaneous fat stripped from underlying fascia** — Common in industrial/RTA injuries. Open vs closed (Morel-Lavallée lesion). High risk of skin necrosis; needs early debridement/skin grafting.
</details>

## Spine

### Q22 — First radiological feature in TB spine

First radiological finding in tuberculosis of the spine?

- A. Vertebral collapse
- B. Paravertebral abscess
- C. Loss of curvature
- D. Disc space narrowing

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Loss of curvature** — Earliest sign (kyphosis or loss of lordosis). Disc space narrowing follows. Late: vertebral collapse, gibbus deformity, paravertebral cold abscess.
</details>

## Bone Tumours

### Q24 — Giant cell tumour at knee

30-year-old female with knee tumour; biopsy shows multinucleated giant cells. Diagnosis?

- A. Osteosarcoma
- B. Ewing's sarcoma
- C. Osteoblastoma
- D. Giant cell tumour (osteoclastoma)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Giant cell tumour** — Around knee (distal femur > proximal tibia); 20–40 years; eccentric, lytic, "soap-bubble" appearance, no marginal sclerosis. Locally aggressive; treated with curettage + bone graft.
</details>

### Q25 — Osteoclastoma at wrist (soap bubble)

30-year-old male with wrist swelling for 3 months; X-ray shows eccentric lytic lesion with soap-bubble appearance. Diagnosis?

- A. Ewing's sarcoma
- B. Osteosarcoma
- C. Osteochondroma
- D. Osteoclastoma (GCT)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Osteoclastoma** — Distal radius is the third most common location for GCT (after distal femur and proximal tibia). Soap-bubble lytic lesion in epiphyseal-metaphyseal region.
</details>

### Q26 — Multiple enchondromas (Ollier disease)

Woman with multiple benign cartilage tumours in hand bones on X-ray. Diagnosis?

- A. Brown tumours
- B. Fibrous dysplasia
- C. Multiple exostoses
- D. Multiple enchondromas (Ollier disease)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Multiple enchondromas (Ollier disease)** — Non-hereditary; multiple cartilage tumours in tubular bones; can transform to chondrosarcoma. With haemangiomas = Maffucci syndrome.
</details>

### Q27 — Brodie's abscess in 12-year-old

12-year-old with leg swelling for 6 months, X-ray shows well-defined lytic lesion with sclerotic border. Diagnosis?

- A. Osteosarcoma
- B. Osteoblastoma
- C. Ewing's sarcoma
- D. Brodie's abscess

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Brodie's abscess** — Chronic localised osteomyelitis; sclerotic-rimmed lytic lesion in metaphysis (commonly proximal tibia). Usually S. aureus. Treated with antibiotics ± surgical drainage.
</details>

### Q28 — Rim sign in chronic osteomyelitis

"Rim sign" on imaging is seen in:

- A. Osteosarcoma
- B. Ewing's sarcoma
- C. Giant cell tumour
- D. Chronic osteomyelitis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Chronic osteomyelitis** — Sequestrum (dead bone) surrounded by involucrum (new bone formation) with a sclerotic rim. Sinus tracts may be present.
</details>

### Q29 — GCT variants differentiated by

Giant cell tumour variants are differentiated by:

- A. Clinical presentation
- B. Radiographic features
- C. Tissue biopsy (histology)
- D. Genetic testing

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Tissue biopsy** — Histopathology distinguishes benign GCT, GCT with malignant transformation, and "brown tumours" (hyperparathyroidism), aneurysmal bone cyst, chondroblastoma.

## Knee Pathology

### Q30 — ACL injury tests

Soccer player with positive Lachman and anterior drawer test:

- A. MCL
- B. LCL
- C. ACL
- D. PCL

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. ACL (anterior cruciate ligament)** — Lachman is the most sensitive test (30° flexion, hands on femur and tibia). Anterior drawer (90° flexion) also positive. Pivot shift test confirms rotational instability.
</details>

---

### Q31 — Valgus stress test

Stress test shown for medial knee stability:

- A. EKG stress test
- B. Treadmill stress test
- C. Valgus stress test
- D. Varus stress test

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Valgus stress test** — Tests medial collateral ligament (MCL); applied with knee at 30° flexion. Varus stress test = LCL.
</details>

### Q32 — Pseudogout (rhomboid, positively birefringent)

45-year-old man, joint discomfort, rhomboid, positively birefringent crystals. Diagnosis?

- A. Gout
- B. Rheumatoid arthritis
- C. Osteoarthritis
- D. Pseudogout (CPPD)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Pseudogout (CPPD)** — Calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate crystals: rhomboid, weakly positively birefringent. Gout: needle-shaped, strongly negatively birefringent monosodium urate.
</details>

## Nerve Injury

### Q33 — Neurotmesis

Complete severance of a peripheral nerve is called:

- A. Neurapraxia
- B. Axonotmesis
- C. Neurotmesis
- D. Neuroma

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Neurotmesis** — Seddon's classification: neurapraxia (focal demyelination, conduction block) → axonotmesis (axon disrupted, endoneurium intact) → neurotmesis (complete severance; needs surgical repair).
</details>

---

### Q34 — Pen test for median nerve

The "Pen test" assesses:

- A. Posterior interosseous nerve
- B. Ulnar nerve
- C. Musculocutaneous nerve
- D. Median nerve

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Median nerve** — Pen test: patient touches the pen held above the thumb in radial abduction; tests abductor pollicis brevis (median nerve, recurrent branch). Failure = carpal tunnel/median nerve injury.
</details>

---

### Q35 — End-to-side neurorrhaphy

End-to-side neurorrhaphy of cutaneous nerve is used for:

- A. Motor regeneration
- B. Sensory regeneration
- C. Mixed motor and sensory
- D. Autonomic only

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Mixed motor and sensory neuron regeneration** — Axons from a donor nerve sprout collaterally into the side of a recipient nerve to restore sensation/motor function without sacrificing donor function.
</details>

## Miscellaneous

### Q36 — Cobb's angle

Method of scoliosis measurement on X-ray:

- A. Bohler's angle
- B. Ferguson angle
- C. Bauman's angle
- D. Cobb's angle

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Cobb's angle** — Angle between lines perpendicular to the most tilted vertebrae at the upper and lower ends of the curve. > 10° = scoliosis; > 50° in adults often needs surgery.
</details>

### Q37 — External fixator advantage in open fracture

Main advantage of external fixator in open fracture:

- A. Promotes bone healing
- B. Minimises infection risk
- C. Allows early ROM
- D. Stabilises broken bone

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Stabilises broken bone** — External fixation provides stabilisation while avoiding placement of metalwork in contaminated wound; reduces infection risk indirectly.
</details>

Gower sign represents:

- A. Inability to walk on tiptoes
