# Surgery, Taught Through Its Questions

### A concept-first companion to the NEET-PG General Surgery question pool

> Built from the 256-question Surgery pool (NEET-PG 2015–2025). This is not a list of answers. It is a book that takes each recurring question, opens it up, and shows you the surgical principle hiding inside — so that when the examiner changes the wording next year, the question still belongs to you.

---

## How to Read This Book

Most students revise surgery by memorising answers. That works until the examiner rotates the stem — same concept, new patient, new distractors — and the memorised answer no longer fits. This book is designed to defeat exactly that trick.

Every topic here is built the same way. First we name the **pattern** the examiner keeps using. Then we uncover the **concept** that generates every version of that pattern. Then we deliberately walk through the **wrong options** — because in a well-set MCQ the wrong options are not random; they are the specific mistakes the examiner expects you to make. Finally we compress the whole thing into a **one-line anchor** you can recall under time pressure.

Read it with the questions open beside you. Attempt first, reveal second. The goal is not to recognise these 256 questions. The goal is to recognise the 256 *concepts* so completely that the next 256 questions feel familiar.

### The mindset that scores marks

Surgery is a high-weight subject: expect 10 or more questions per paper, and the modern trend is clinical vignettes and images, not one-liners. That is good news — vignettes reward understanding and punish rote learning. If you know *why* Courvoisier's law works, you can answer it whether the stem says "palpable gallbladder," shows you a photograph, or buries it inside a jaundice differential.

### The negative-marking arithmetic (read this once, live by it forever)

NEET-PG scores +4 for correct, −1 for wrong, 0 for blank. Run the expected value:

- **Blind guess (4 options):** (¼ × +4) + (¾ × −1) = **+0.25.** Marginally positive — a pure blind guess still helps you.
- **One option eliminated (3 left):** (⅓ × +4) + (⅔ × −1) = **+0.66.**
- **Two options eliminated (2 left):** (½ × +4) + (½ × −1) = **+1.5.**

The rule that falls out of this: **attempt every question where you can eliminate even one option.** This book spends as much effort teaching you *how to eliminate* as teaching you the right answer, because elimination is where marks are actually won.

# Chapter 1 — Breast Surgery

## 1.1 Peau d'orange: read the skin, name the lymphatic

**The pattern.** A middle-aged woman, a painless progressive breast lump, and a photograph or phrase describing "orange-peel" skin. The question asks for the *cause* of the skin change — and the trap is an option list full of anatomically plausible structures.

**The concept.** Peau d'orange is lymphoedema of the breast skin. Tumour emboli block the **subdermal lymphatics**, so the skin between the hair follicles and sweat-gland openings swells — but at each follicle the skin stays tethered down. Swollen skin punctuated by tethered pits is precisely the surface of an orange. Everything you need is in the word "lymphatic."

**Why the tempting wrongs are wrong.** *Cooper's ligament involvement* produces skin **dimpling/retraction**, not orange-peel — a different sign entirely, and the examiner offers it to see whether you can separate two skin signs. *Lactiferous duct infiltration* gives nipple changes and discharge. *Anterior chest wall spread* is a late fixation phenomenon (T4a), not the mechanism of the skin texture.

**The variation that catches people.** The same photograph reappears asking for **TNM stage**. Skin oedema over one-third or more of the breast with a diffuse "inflammatory" look is **T4d — inflammatory breast cancer**, staged at least IIIB when non-metastatic. (One item in the pool tags it "Stage IVD," which is not standard nomenclature — read it as *T4d, at minimum Stage IIIB, higher if metastatic.*) So the skin sign is a doorway to two different questions: mechanism (subdermal lymphatics) and stage (T4d).

**Memory anchor.** *Orange peel = subdermal lymphatics blocked; dimpling = Cooper's ligament pulled.*

**Rapid revision.** Two skin signs, two mechanisms — never confuse the blocked lymphatic (peau d'orange) with the tugged ligament (dimpling).

## 1.2 The breast-lump differential by age and feel

**The pattern.** A woman is given with an age, a size, and a texture; you choose the diagnosis. The examiner is testing whether you let *age and size* drive the answer.

**The concept.** Let the demographics decide:

- A **large (5–6 cm), rapidly enlarging** mass in a **40-something** woman with a smooth, bulky, "leaf-like" histology is a **phyllodes tumour** — think of it as a fibroadenoma that kept growing and turned aggressive. Size and age separate it from the small, mobile fibroadenoma of the young woman.
- A discrete mobile lump in a woman in her twenties is a **fibroadenoma**.
- Tenderness, erythema, and a lactating history point to **mastitis/abscess**, not neoplasm.

**How to eliminate.** When the mass is *big and older*, cross out fibroadenoma (wrong age, wrong size) and mastitis (no inflammation). Phyllodes survives.

**Memory anchor.** *Big leafy lump in a 40s woman = phyllodes.*

## 1.3 Metastatic and biology-driven management

**The pattern.** A breast cancer stem hands you a receptor status or a metastatic site and asks for management. The concept being tested is that **modern breast-cancer treatment follows biology, not just anatomy.**

**The concept.** **HER2-positive disease with visceral (liver) metastases** is treated with **anti-HER2 targeted therapy (trastuzumab) plus chemotherapy**, and surgery becomes palliative rather than curative. You do not lead with resection of metastases; you lead with systemic biological therapy because the disease is systemic.

**Two drug-oncology hooks the pool tests here.** *Cyclophosphamide* → its metabolite **acrolein** injures the bladder → haemorrhagic cystitis and later **transitional cell carcinoma of the bladder** (prevent with **mesna**). *Tamoxifen* → **endometrial carcinoma**. Keep the pair straight: cyclophosphamide points down to the bladder, tamoxifen points to the uterus.

**Van Nuys — the DCIS score.** The Van Nuys Prognostic Index for ductal carcinoma in situ is built from **size, margin width, grade (with comedonecrosis), and age** — deliberately *not* ER status. When the examiner asks "which is NOT part of Van Nuys," the answer is **ER status**, because Van Nuys predicts local recurrence of in-situ disease, a question of geometry and grade, not hormone receptors.

**Memory anchor.** *HER2+ → trastuzumab first. Van Nuys = Size, Margin, Grade, Age — no ER. Cyclophosphamide→bladder, Tamoxifen→endometrium.*

**Chapter 1 recap.** Skin signs encode mechanism (subdermal lymphatics vs Cooper's ligament). Age and size drive the lump differential (phyllodes is the big, older fibroadenoma-gone-wrong). Management follows biology (HER2 → trastuzumab). And two exam-favourite drugs each have a target organ you must not swap.

# Chapter 2 — Endocrine Surgery: Thyroid, Parathyroid, Adrenal, and the Neuroendocrine Family

## 2.1 The post-thyroidectomy patient who tingles

**The pattern.** This is one of the most-repeated stems in the entire pool — it appears at least four times across years. *Two to three days after thyroidectomy, perioral numbness (or tingling of the fingers).* Which investigation?

**The concept.** During thyroidectomy the **parathyroid glands** can be inadvertently removed, bruised, or devascularised. Low PTH → **hypocalcaemia** → neuromuscular irritability → perioral tingling, then Chvostek's and Trousseau's signs, and in severe cases tetany or laryngospasm. So the required test is **serum calcium, phosphate, and PTH** — never thyroid function tests.

**Why the wrongs are wrong.** *Free T3/T4* and *T3/T4/TSH* are the reflex answers for anyone who reads only the word "thyroidectomy" and stops thinking. But thyroid hormone derangement does not cause acute perioral numbness on day 3 — hypocalcaemia does. *Radioiodine scan* is irrelevant to an acute metabolic complication.

**The elimination logic.** Match the *symptom* to the *gland*. Numbness and tingling are calcium symptoms → parathyroid → check calcium. The word "thyroidectomy" is the setting, not the answer.

**Memory anchor.** *Tingling after thyroid surgery = the parathyroids are crying. Check calcium, not thyroid.*

## 2.2 Where the parathyroids go when they leave home

**The pattern.** During surgery a parathyroid is autotransplanted — to which muscle?

**The concept.** The standard site is the **brachioradialis of the non-dominant forearm** (or sternocleidomastoid). The forearm is chosen because it is superficial and accessible: if hyperparathyroidism recurs from the graft, you can re-explore it under local anaesthesia, and you can even sample PTH from that arm to prove the graft is the culprit. It is a deliberately *retrievable* location.

**Memory anchor.** *Parathyroid graft goes to the forearm you can watch — brachioradialis, non-dominant side.*

## 2.3 Hyperparathyroidism reads its signature on bone and skull

**The pattern.** "Salt-and-pepper pot skull," brown tumours, subperiosteal resorption — name the condition. Or the inverse: which bone lesion does hypoparathyroidism cause?

**The concept.** **Hyperparathyroidism** is a disease of *too much resorption*. Osteoclasts, driven indirectly by PTH, chew bone: you get subperiosteal resorption (classically radial border of the phalanges), brown tumours (osteitis fibrosa cystica), and the mottled **salt-and-pepper skull**. **Hypoparathyroidism** causes *no* characteristic destructive bone lesion — so when the stem asks "effect of hypoparathyroidism on bone," the answer is **none of the above**. The trap is symmetry: students expect hypoparathyroidism to produce a mirror-image bone lesion. It does not.

**The physiology that explains it (and is itself tested).** PTH receptors sit on **osteoblasts, not osteoclasts**. PTH tells the osteoblast to release **RANKL**, and RANKL then activates the osteoclast. That is why "osteoclasts have a PTH receptor" is false, and why "PTH acts directly on osteoblasts" is true. The osteoclast does the demolition, but it takes its orders second-hand.

**Memory anchor.** *Hyper-PTH eats bone (salt-and-pepper skull, brown tumours). PTH knocks on the osteoBlast's door; the osteoBlast sends RANKL to wake the osteoClast.*

## 2.4 RET, MEN, and the medullary thyroid link

**The pattern.** "RET proto-oncogene is associated with…" (appears twice). Or a MEN syndrome asking where a tumour sits.

**The concept.** **RET → medullary carcinoma of the thyroid** (parafollicular C cells, calcitonin), the thyroid component of **MEN 2A and 2B**. Anchor RET to the C cell.

**MEN 1's gastrinoma — location matters.** In MEN 1, gastrinomas most often sit in the **duodenum** (not the pancreas as many assume), within the **gastrinoma triangle** (cystic duct above, junction of 2nd/3rd duodenum below, pancreatic neck medially). MEN-1 duodenal gastrinomas are typically **small and multiple** — which is exactly why they are hard to find and easy to miss. This stem also repeats across years, always answered **duodenum**.

**Memory anchor.** *RET rides the C cell (medullary thyroid). MEN 1 gastrinoma lives in the duodenal triangle — small and multiple.*

## 2.5 Phaeochromocytoma: block alpha before you touch it

**The pattern.** A patient with headaches, paroxysmal hypertension, palpitations, and raised urinary **VMA/metanephrines**, with a retroperitoneal (adrenal) mass. Two questions get asked: preoperative drug, and associated syndromes.

**The concept — the preop drug.** Give **phenoxybenzamine**, an irreversible **alpha-blocker**, for 10–14 days before surgery. The tumour is a catecholamine bomb; handling it releases a surge. If you block beta first, you leave alpha-mediated vasoconstriction unopposed → hypertensive crisis. So the sacred rule is **alpha before beta, always.**

**The syndromes it belongs to.** Phaeochromocytoma associates with **MEN 2A/2B, von Hippel–Lindau, and hereditary paraganglioma-phaeochromocytoma syndromes** — and with **neurofibromatosis type 1** (not type 2). So "which is NOT associated" = **NF2**. Remember NF1 (café-au-lait, peripheral neurofibromas) rides with phaeo; NF2 (bilateral acoustic neuromas) does not.

**Memory anchor.** *Phaeo: alpha-block (phenoxybenzamine) before beta. Rides with MEN2, VHL, NF1 — never NF2.*

## 2.6 The thyroid nodule and the retrosternal goitre

**The solitary nodule — first test.** A new solitary thyroid nodule: the **first** investigation is **TSH/T3/T4** (thyroid function), because the whole management pathway forks on whether the nodule is *functioning*. A hot (toxic) nodule almost never harbours cancer and heads toward radioiodine; a cold nodule needs FNAC. You order function first, then image/aspirate. (FNAC is the most important *diagnostic* test for the nodule itself, but function tests come *first* to triage.)

**The retrosternal goitre — surgical access.** Around **95% of retrosternal goitres can be delivered through a cervical (collar) neck incision**, because their blood supply comes from the neck (inferior thyroid artery). Sternotomy is reserved for the rare *true* intrathoracic goitre with independent mediastinal blood supply. So "sternotomy is always required" is false, and "most are removed through the neck" is the answer.

**Memory anchor.** *Solitary nodule → check TSH first. Retrosternal goitre → neck incision usually suffices (blood supply is cervical).*

**Chapter 2 recap.** Endocrine surgery rewards the student who matches symptom to gland: tingling → parathyroid → calcium; RET → C cell → medullary thyroid; VMA → phaeo → alpha-block first. Hyperparathyroidism destroys bone while hypoparathyroidism spares it, and PTH always works *through* the osteoblast. Grafts go to the forearm so you can watch them, and most retrosternal goitres never need the chest opened.

# Chapter 3 — Hepatobiliary and Pancreatic Surgery

## 3.1 Courvoisier's law: painless jaundice + palpable gallbladder = think malignancy

**The pattern.** A man with **painless** jaundice and a **palpable, non-tender mass in the right hypochondrium** — or the epigastric variant with pale stools and dark urine. The examiner wants the diagnosis, and sometimes the *unlikely* diagnosis.

**The concept.** Courvoisier's law: in a jaundiced patient, a **palpable gallbladder is unlikely to be due to stones**. Stones cause chronic cholecystitis, which makes the gallbladder **fibrotic and contracted** — it cannot distend. So a *distensible, palpable* gallbladder with obstructive jaundice implies obstruction **distal to the cystic duct by something other than a stone** — a **periampullary / pancreatic head / ampulla of Vater carcinoma**. The obstruction is slow, silent, and malignant, hence *painless*.

**How to use it both ways.** When asked for the diagnosis → **carcinoma ampulla / pancreatic head**. When asked which diagnosis is **unlikely** in an obstructive-jaundice-with-mass picture → **chronic cholecystitis**, precisely because that gallbladder would be shrunken and impalpable.

**The classic exception (worth a sentence).** Courvoisier's law "fails" when both a stone and a distended gallbladder coexist (e.g. a stone in Hartmann's pouch causing a mucocoele, or double pathology) — but for the exam, the law points you at malignancy.

**Memory anchor.** *Painless jaundice + palpable gallbladder = malignant obstruction (periampullary), because stones give a shrunken gallbladder.*

## 3.2 Acute pancreatitis: the enzyme you diagnose with vs the markers you prognose with

This is the single densest concept-cluster in the pool — nearly a dozen questions orbit acute pancreatitis. Group them and the whole thing collapses into four ideas.

**Idea 1 — Diagnosis: lipase beats amylase.** **Serum lipase** is more **sensitive and specific**; it rises in 4–8 h, peaks at 24 h, and stays up for 8–14 days. Amylase is less specific (rises in salivary disease, perforation, ectopic pregnancy) and normalises quickly. As a rapid non-invasive **screen**, **urinary trypsinogen-2** dipstick is the early marker. In **chronic** pancreatitis, where the gland has burnt out, amylase may be normal — **lipase / faecal elastase** still reflect residual acinar function.

**Idea 2 — Prognosis: amylase tells you nothing.** The killer trap: **serum amylase is NOT a prognostic marker.** Its *level does not track severity*. The prognostic scores (Ranson, Glasgow) use **calcium (low), glucose (high), AST, LDH, WBC, age, urea, PaO₂** — never amylase. So "which is NOT a prognostic factor" = **amylase**.

**Idea 3 — Causes and the calcium paradox.** Causes are the familiar GET SMASHED list. Two exam points: **hypercalcaemia** *causes* pancreatitis, so during an attack you expect calcium to fall (saponification), which is why *"acute pancreatitis causes hypercalcaemia" is the false statement* — the relationship runs the other way. And **hypertriglyceridaemia, pancreas divisum, and sphincter of Oddi dysfunction** can each cause **recurrent** attacks "without obvious pathology," which is why the honest answer to that stem is **all of the above**.

**Idea 4 — Complications you can see and name.** **Cullen's sign** (periumbilical bruising) and Grey-Turner's sign (flank bruising) mark **haemorrhagic** pancreatitis — retroperitoneal blood tracking to the skin. **ARDS** with **bilateral infiltrates** on chest X-ray is the refractory-hypoxia complication (cytokine-driven alveolar damage). A **pseudocyst** needs **≥4 weeks** to form its fibrous (non-epithelial) wall; before that it is just an acute fluid collection.

**Memory anchor.** *Lipase to diagnose, amylase never to prognose. Hypercalcaemia causes pancreatitis (calcium then falls). Pseudocyst = 4 weeks. Cullen = haemorrhage. Bilateral infiltrates = ARDS.*

## 3.3 Chronic pancreatitis and the head-of-pancreas cancer work-up

**Chronic pancreatitis** in adults is most commonly caused by **chronic alcohol** use; its plain-film / ERCP signature is the **"chain of lakes"** appearance — a beaded, alternately dilated and strictured main pancreatic duct. **Carcinoma head of pancreas** is best confirmed by **tissue** — image-guided **biopsy/FNA** (EUS-guided in practice) — because you must have histology before a Whipple or palliation decision.

**Memory anchor.** *Alcohol → chronic pancreatitis → chain of lakes. Head-of-pancreas cancer → get tissue (guided biopsy).*

## 3.4 Liver: abscess logic and the AFP alarm

**Amoebic liver abscess.** A patient with diarrhoea and a **single right-lobe** abscess: treat **medically with metronidazole** (cure >90%), then a **luminal agent** (paromomycin) for the carrier state. Aspiration/drainage is *not* first-line — reserve it for diagnostic doubt, no response in 48–72 h, a large (>5 cm) or **left-lobe** abscess (risk of pericardial rupture), or impending rupture. The intra-peritoneal abscess that forms after generalised contamination most commonly settles in the **subhepatic** space (and the subphrenic/pelvic spaces) — gravity and peritoneal reflections decide the address.

**The AFP alarm.** A cirrhotic/alcoholic with weight loss and a markedly raised **alpha-fetoprotein (e.g. 600 ng/mL)** should scream **hepatocellular carcinoma** — that is the concept to *learn*, whatever an individual answer key says. (One pool item labels such a vignette "alcoholic hepatitis"; treat that as a data-entry slip and hold onto the rule: **AFP in the hundreds + cirrhosis = HCC until proven otherwise.**)

**Memory anchor.** *Amoebic abscess → metronidazole first, drain only if big/left/refractory. High AFP + cirrhosis → HCC.*

**Chapter 3 recap.** One law (Courvoisier) turns painless jaundice into a malignancy hunt. Pancreatitis is four ideas: lipase diagnoses, amylase never prognoses, hypercalcaemia is a *cause* not a *result*, and the named signs (Cullen, ARDS, 4-week pseudocyst) are just complications with addresses. Alcohol writes "chain of lakes" on the duct, and a soaring AFP writes "HCC" on the liver.

# Chapter 4 — Foregut: Oesophagus, Stomach, and the Salivary Detour

## 4.1 Zenker's diverticulum and Dohlman's procedure

**The pattern.** "Dohlman's procedure is for…?" (appears three times — clearly a favourite).

**The concept.** **Dohlman's** = endoscopic division of the **cricopharyngeal bar** (the common wall between a **Zenker's pharyngeal pouch** and the oesophagus), effectively an endoscopic cricopharyngeal myotomy. Zenker's is a *pulsion* diverticulum through **Killian's dehiscence** (the weak point between thyropharyngeus and cricopharyngeus). Symptoms: dysphagia, regurgitation of undigested food, halitosis, a gurgling neck.

**Memory anchor.** *Dohlman splits the bar of a Zenker's pouch.*

## 4.2 Achalasia by manometry (Chicago types)

High-resolution manometry gives **failed LES relaxation** in all achalasia, and the *body* pattern names the type:

- **Type I** — no contractility (aperistalsis).
- **Type II** — **panoesophageal pressurisation** (best prognosis, best response to therapy).
- **Type III** — **spastic**: premature, simultaneous, high-amplitude distal contractions.

So "massive simultaneous high-pressure distal contractions" = **Type III**.

**Memory anchor.** *Achalasia: I = nothing, II = pressurised, III = spastic.*

## 4.3 The caustic-ingestion airway-to-gut decision

**The pattern.** A young patient swallows a strong alkali (e.g. 90% NaOH), now has complete dysphagia.

**The concept.** Alkali causes **liquefactive necrosis** with deep penetration. In the acute setting with complete oesophageal obstruction/injury, you must **not** attempt oesophageal reconstruction on inflamed friable tissue and you must secure **nutrition** — a **feeding jejunostomy** provides enteral access while the oesophagus is out of action, deferring definitive reconstruction until the injury declares itself. Acute stenting/anastomosis on a fresh caustic burn invites perforation.

**Memory anchor.** *Fresh caustic stricture with complete dysphagia → feeding jejunostomy first; reconstruct later.*

## 4.4 Oesophageal perforation and the contrast you choose

To image a suspected **oesophageal perforation**, the pool's answer is **water-soluble** thinking in spirit — but note the item lists **barium sulphate** as keyed. The teaching principle: use a **water-soluble contrast (e.g. Gastrografin/iohexol) first** because barium spilled into the mediastinum causes mediastinitis; barium is used *only if* the water-soluble study is negative and you still suspect a leak (barium is more sensitive for small perforations). Know the principle (water-soluble first) and the nuance (barium for the small occult leak) and you can defend either phrasing.

**Memory anchor.** *Suspected oesophageal perforation → water-soluble contrast first; barium only for the occult small leak.*

## 4.5 Post-gastrectomy nutrition and GERD surgery

**After gastrectomy, supplement vitamin B12.** Removing the stomach removes the **parietal cells** that make **intrinsic factor**; without IF, B12 cannot be absorbed in the terminal ileum → megaloblastic anaemia over years. (Distal gastrectomy still sacrifices enough parietal-cell mass to warrant this.)

**Refractory GERD → 360° Nissen fundoplication.** The full wrap is the standard antireflux operation; partial wraps (Toupet 270°) are chosen when oesophageal motility is poor. The pool keys the **360° Nissen**.

**Cushing's ulcer → head injury; Curling's ulcer → burns.** Keep the pair straight: **Cu**shing = **C**ranial (raised ICP drives vagal acid output), **Cur**ling = **C**utaneous burns. The pool tests Cushing = head injury directly.

**Memory anchor.** *Gastrectomy → B12 (lost intrinsic factor). Refractory GERD → Nissen 360°. Cushing=Cranial, Curling=Cutaneous(burns).*

## 4.6 The salivary and neck-mass detour

Two foregut-adjacent favourites live here because of shared anatomy:

**Sialolithiasis.** **Mealtime pain and swelling** on one side, a stone in **Wharton's duct** on ultrasound → **submandibular sialolithiasis**. The submandibular gland is the commonest site because its secretions are **viscous** and its duct runs **uphill** against gravity. Most stones are radio-opaque.

**Bilateral parotid enlargement** occurs in Sjögren's, HIV (DILS), and chronic pancreatitis/alcoholism — but **not SLE** (which gives lymphadenopathy/serositis). So "all cause bilateral parotidomegaly EXCEPT" = **SLE**.

**Memory anchor.** *Mealtime pain + Wharton's duct stone = submandibular sialolithiasis. Bilateral parotids swell in everything but SLE.*

**Chapter 4 recap.** Dohlman splits a Zenker's bar; Chicago numbers the achalasia; caustic burns get a feeding jejunostomy before any reconstruction; gastrectomy costs you B12; and two eponymous stress ulcers sort themselves by their first letters.

# Chapter 5 — Colorectal, Anorectal, and Small-Bowel Surgery

## 5.1 Diverticular perforation: the Hinchey ladder

**The pattern.** "Diverticular perforation with **faecal peritonitis** at laparotomy — which Hinchey stage?" (appears twice).

**The concept.** Hinchey climbs from contained to catastrophic: **I** = pericolic abscess, **II** = pelvic/distant abscess, **III** = **purulent** peritonitis, **IV** = **faecal** peritonitis. Faecal contamination is the top of the ladder → **Stage IV**, which classically needs a **Hartmann's procedure** (resect, end colostomy, close the rectal stump) because anastomosing bowel in a faecal-soiled field will leak.

**Memory anchor.** *Hinchey IV = faeces everywhere = Hartmann's.*

## 5.2 Stomas and the Hartmann's logic

**Hartmann's procedure** forms an **end colostomy** with an oversewn rectal stump — the go-to for **obstructed or perforated left colon** when a primary anastomosis is unsafe. Learn *why* the stoma exists: you are diverting faeces away from a segment you dare not join. Contraindications to **siting** a stoma are technical, not demographic: avoid **bony prominences, skin creases, and old scars**, and beware the poorly motivated patient — but **age >60 is not a contraindication.**

**Memory anchor.** *Hartmann's = end colostomy for the unsafe left colon. Stoma siting avoids scars/creases/bones — age doesn't matter.*

## 5.3 Anal canal: the dentate line decides everything

The **dentate (pectinate) line** is the great watershed of the anal canal, and the pool tests it from two directions:

- **Lymphatic drainage.** **Below** the dentate line (anal margin, perianal skin) drains to **superficial inguinal nodes**; **above** it drains to **internal iliac / superior rectal (para-aortic)** nodes. So a carcinoma near the anal opening spreads to **superficial inguinal nodes**.
- **Treatment of anal SCC.** The **Nigro regimen** — **5-FU + mitomycin-C + pelvic radiotherapy** — is the chemoradiation protocol that made **abdominoperineal resection** unnecessary as first-line for anal squamous carcinoma. It is one of surgery's great organ-preservation stories.

**Memory anchor.** *Dentate line: below → inguinal nodes, above → internal iliac. Anal SCC → Nigro (5-FU + mitomycin + RT), not the knife.*

## 5.4 APR and the nerve you must respect

**The pattern.** "Most common complication after ligating the **first vessel** (the IMA) during **abdominoperineal resection**." (appears twice).

**The concept.** High ligation of the **inferior mesenteric artery** endangers the **superior hypogastric (sympathetic) plexus** lying just anterior to the aortic bifurcation. **Sympathetic** injury → **retrograde ejaculation + bladder dysfunction** (the sympathetics close the bladder neck during ejaculation; lose them and semen goes backward). Contrast with **parasympathetic (nervi erigentes)** injury lower in the pelvis → **impotence** (erectile failure). So the keyed answer is **sympathetic: retrograde ejaculation + bladder dysfunction.**

**Memory anchor.** *IMA ligation hits the sympathetic plexus → retrograde ejaculation + bladder dysfunction (sympathetics = "shoot," parasympathetics = "point").*

## 5.5 The anorectal-sepsis trio

Three benign perianal conditions the pool keeps rotating:

- **Recurrent discharge and pain around the anus for years** → **fistula-in-ano** (a chronic epithelialised track, often post-abscess).
- **Painful swelling in the natal cleft / gluteal region, classically in a hairy young man who sits and drives** (the "jeep driver") → **pilonidal sinus** (hair-driven midline pits).
- The examiner separates these from a simple **boil/carbuncle** by *chronicity and site*: fistula = recurrent + a track; pilonidal = natal cleft + hair + sitting occupation.

**Memory anchor.** *Recurrent perianal discharge = fistula-in-ano. Hairy driver with a natal-cleft abscess = pilonidal sinus.*

## 5.6 Colonic odds and ends that examiners love

- **Most common site of colorectal carcinoma = rectum** (prolonged mucosal contact with stored stool).
- **Rigler's (double-wall) sign** = gas on *both* sides of the bowel wall = **pneumoperitoneum**.
- **Crohn's vs UC discriminators:** Crohn's is **skip lesions** (not continuous), **non-caseating granulomas**, **cobblestoning**, **rectal sparing**, transmural. So "continuous lesion on endoscopy" is the statement that is **false for Crohn's** (continuity belongs to UC).
- **Pseudomembranous colitis:** recurrent diarrhoea + **yellow-white plaques** on colonoscopy after antibiotics = *C. difficile*.
- **Ischaemic colitis** shows **thumbprinting** on imaging (mucosal oedema), classically at the splenic flexure watershed.
- **FAP screening:** after counselling, do **APC gene testing** to identify carriers, then colonoscopic surveillance from puberty.
- **Caecal stab injury:** prefer **right hemicolectomy with ileo-transverse anastomosis** — the thin-walled, tenuously perfused caecum repairs poorly.
- **Inferior mesenteric artery branches:** **left colic, sigmoid, superior rectal** — so "sigmoid artery" is the IMA branch (middle colic and right colic are SMA territory).

**Memory anchor.** *Rectum = commonest CRC. Rigler = free gas. Crohn skips (UC is continuous). C. diff = pseudomembranes. IMA = Left colic + Sigmoid + Superior rectal.*

**Chapter 5 recap.** Hinchey climbs to faecal peritonitis and a Hartmann's; the dentate line splits lymph drainage and hands anal SCC to Nigro; IMA ligation threatens the sympathetic plexus (retrograde ejaculation); and a fistula, a pilonidal sinus, and a pseudomembrane each have a signature history.

# Chapter 6 — Hernia and the Abdominal Wall

## 6.1 Direct vs indirect: the inferior epigastric vessels are the referee

**The pattern.** A hernia is defined by its relation to the **inferior epigastric vessels**; name it and its repair.

**The concept.** **Medial** to the inferior epigastric vessels (through **Hesselbach's triangle**) = **direct** inguinal hernia; **lateral** (through the deep ring) = **indirect**. The direct hernia pushes through a floor defended only by the **conjoint tendon and transversalis fascia** — so "weakness of the conjoint tendon" is the structural cause the pool asks for. The modern repair of choice for either is the **Lichtenstein tension-free mesh** repair.

**Memory anchor.** *Medial to epigastric vessels = Direct (through weak conjoint tendon). Lateral = Indirect. Repair = Lichtenstein mesh.*

## 6.2 Nerve entrapment after hernia repair

After **laparoscopic** inguinal hernia repair, **lateral thigh pain** points to entrapment of the **lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh** (it pierces near the tacks/staples in the "triangle of pain," lateral to the vessels). Distinguish from the **ilioinguinal/iliohypogastric** nerves (open-repair groin/scrotal pain) and the **femoral** nerve (motor, quadriceps). The laparoscopic clue + *lateral thigh* location fixes the answer.

**Memory anchor.** *Lap hernia + lateral thigh pain = lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh entrapment.*

## 6.3 Umbilical hernia that turns painful and irreducible

An umbilical hernia that was reducible for years and now becomes **painful and non-reducible** is **obstructed/incarcerated** and needs **surgery (excision + repair, mesh hernioplasty)** — conservative antibiotics or incision-and-drainage are wrong because this is a mechanical, not infective, problem.

**Memory anchor.** *Was reducible, now painful + irreducible = operate (it's obstructed), don't drain.*

## 6.4 Congenital wall and duct defects

- **Congenital diaphragmatic hernia:** the commonest is the **left posterolateral (Bochdalek)** hernia — a neonate with respiratory distress and bowel in the chest.
- **Faecal discharge from the umbilicus in a baby** = **patent vitellointestinal (omphalomesenteric) duct** (bowel-to-umbilicus fistula). Contrast a **urachal** anomaly, which leaks **urine** from the umbilicus. The *content* of the discharge names the duct.

**Memory anchor.** *CDH = left Bochdalek. Faeces from umbilicus = patent vitellointestinal duct; urine = urachus.*

**Chapter 6 recap.** The inferior epigastric vessels referee direct vs indirect; the conjoint tendon fails in direct hernias; lap repair can trap the lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh; a newly painful irreducible hernia is a surgical emergency; and congenital leaks are named by what drips out.

# Chapter 7 — Vascular Surgery

## 7.1 Leriche syndrome: the aortoiliac triad

**The pattern.** A male smoker with **bilateral buttock/thigh claudication**, **impotence**, and **absent/diminished femoral pulses** — appears at least three times, sometimes as "where is the lesion?"

**The concept.** The triad localises disease to the **aortoiliac segment (aortic bifurcation)** — **Leriche syndrome**. Buttock claudication and erectile failure both require internal iliac flow, so their combination points *above* the inguinal ligament. Treat with **aortobifemoral bypass** or endovascular stenting. Distinguish from superficial femoral disease (calf claudication, palpable femorals).

**Memory anchor.** *Buttock claudication + impotence + no femoral pulses = Leriche (aortoiliac).*

## 7.2 Aneurysms: the 5.5 cm rule and the dissection tell

**Abdominal aortic aneurysm:** operate on the **asymptomatic** aneurysm at **≥5.5 cm** (or rapid growth >0.5 cm/6 months, or symptoms). Below that, rupture risk is lower than operative risk.

**Aortic dissection:** sudden **tearing chest pain radiating to the back/interscapular region**, a **widened mediastinum**, and an **inter-arm blood-pressure differential** (e.g. 110/90 right vs 160/100 left). Confirm with **CT angiography**. Management forks on Stanford type: **Type A (ascending) → surgery**; **Type B (descending) → medical first**, with **beta-blockade** to drop the shear force (dP/dt) and tight BP control, reserving stenting for complications.

**Memory anchor.** *AAA: operate at 5.5 cm. Dissection: tearing-to-back + inter-arm BP gap + wide mediastinum → CTA; Type B → beta-block first.*

## 7.3 Venous disease: CEAP and the Brodie-Trendelenburg test

**CEAP** classifies **chronic venous disorders** (Clinical–Aetiology–Anatomy–Pathophysiology). On the clinical axis: **C2** varicose veins, **C3** oedema, **C4a** pigmentation/**eczema**, **C4b** lipodermatosclerosis, **C5** healed ulcer, **C6** active ulcer. So "varicose veins **with eczema**" = **C4a**.

**Chronic venous insufficiency** produces **venous eczema, lipodermatosclerosis, haemosiderin pigmentation, and gaiter-area ulcers** — so the true statement is always the one naming these skin changes (compression, not sclerotherapy, is the mainstay).

**Brodie–Trendelenburg test:** compress the saphenofemoral junction, empty the leg, then stand the patient; **rapid filling on releasing the compression = saphenofemoral (SFJ) incompetence**. It localises the leaking valve.

**Memory anchor.** *CEAP C4a = eczema/pigment. Brodie-Trendelenburg rapid refill = SFJ incompetence. CVI = eczema, LDS, gaiter ulcer.*

## 7.4 Gangrene, occupational vasospasm, and bedside Doppler

- **Wet gangrene** = infected, moist, spreading putrefactive necrosis with systemic toxicity (diabetic/vascular). **Dry gangrene** is mummified, demarcated, non-infected. The photo clue is *moist and spreading* vs *dry and lined*.
- **Vibration white finger (hand-arm vibration syndrome):** a worker on **drills/heavy machinery for years** with episodic white fingers → secondary Raynaud's from **vibration exposure**, not from cement or water.
- **Triphasic Doppler waveform** = a **normal** peripheral artery (sharp systolic upstroke, brief diastolic flow reversal, then forward flow). Loss of the triphasic signal (→ biphasic → monophasic) means proximal stenosis. The bedside test giving this waveform is **ultrasound Doppler**.
- **Cirsoid aneurysm:** a **pulsatile, tortuous scalp swelling with a bruit** = an **arteriovenous malformation** of the superficial temporal artery; angiography is diagnostic, treat by embolisation/excision.
- **Fasciotomy** for compartment syndrome must divide **all four layers — skin, subcutaneous tissue, superficial fascia, and deep fascia** — an incomplete release fails.

**Memory anchor.** *Wet=infected/spreading. White fingers in a driller = HAVS. Triphasic = normal artery (Doppler). Pulsatile scalp + bruit = cirsoid aneurysm. Fasciotomy cuts all four layers.*

**Chapter 7 recap.** The aortoiliac triad names Leriche; 5.5 cm triggers AAA repair; dissection announces itself with a tearing pain and an inter-arm gap; CEAP grades venous skin change and Brodie-Trendelenburg finds the leaking junction; and the waveform, the moisture, and the bruit each label a vascular diagnosis at the bedside.

# Chapter 8 — Urology and Male Genital Surgery

## 8.1 The trauma airway of the urinary tract: blood at the meatus

**The pattern.** A motorcycle/RTA victim, **blood at the urethral meatus**, cannot pass urine, haemodynamically stable. Next step? (repeated across years)

**The concept.** Blood at the meatus = **urethral injury until proven otherwise**, and the classic site in blunt trauma is the **membranous urethra** (shearing at the fixed point below the urogenital diaphragm, often with pelvic fracture). The cardinal rule: **do NOT pass a Foley** — you can convert a partial tear into a complete one. **First do a retrograde urethrogram (RUG)** to define the injury; divert with a **suprapubic catheter** if needed.

**Memory anchor.** *Blood at the meatus → RUG before any catheter. Blunt injury = membranous urethra.*

## 8.2 Prostate cancer: when to watch, and how it spreads

**Low-risk disease → active surveillance.** An **elderly** man with **Gleason ≤6, PSA <10, low-volume** tumour is *low-risk*: **active surveillance** (serial PSA, repeat biopsy) avoids the incontinence/impotence of radical treatment for a cancer unlikely to shorten his life. Radical prostatectomy/radiation would be overtreatment.

**Skeletal spread is osteoblastic and venous.** Prostate metastases are classically **osteoblastic (sclerotic)** on X-ray with raised **ALP and PSA** — remember **"Pb = Prostate/Breast = Blastic"** (most other solid tumours are lytic). The **route** to the lumbar vertebrae is the **prostatic venous plexus → Batson's vertebral venous plexus**, a valveless network that lets tumour bypass the caval circulation and seed the spine directly.

**Memory anchor.** *Low-risk elderly prostate cancer → surveillance. Mets are osteoBlastic (Pb) and travel the prostatic/Batson venous plexus to the spine.*

## 8.3 Renal masses: the small RCC and the great imitators

- **RCC <4 cm confined to the kidney → partial (nephron-sparing) nephrectomy.** RCC is **radio- and chemo-resistant**, so surgery is the cure; sparing nephrons protects renal function (essential in solitary kidney, bilateral tumours, or CKD).
- **Putty kidney:** homogeneous **ground-glass calcification** of a non-functioning kidney with **sterile pyuria** = **autonephrectomy of genitourinary tuberculosis**. Sterile pyuria is the golden clue; confirm with early-morning urine AFB.
- **Pelviureteric junction (PUJ) obstruction:** hydronephrosis with a dilated pelvis/calyces and **no contrast in the ureter** on delayed urogram = the commonest congenital upper-tract obstruction; confirm function with a **MAG-3/DTPA** diuretic renogram, treat symptomatic/deteriorating cases with **pyeloplasty**.
- **Autosomal recessive PKD (ARPKD, PKHD1/fibrocystin):** presents in **infancy/childhood** with bilateral "salt-and-pepper" kidneys — so "seen in adults" is the false statement (adult presentation belongs to ADPKD).

**Memory anchor.** *Small RCC → partial nephrectomy (RCC resists radio/chemo). Sterile pyuria + calcified dead kidney = putty kidney (TB). No ureteric contrast = PUJ obstruction. ARPKD = infancy.*

## 8.4 Paediatric urology and the acute scrotum

- **Posterior urethral valves:** an infant boy who cannot pass urine with recurrent episodes and a valve on imaging → **endoscopic destruction/ablation of the valves** is definitive (not repeated catheterisation).
- **Vesicoureteric reflux (VUR):** the commonest cause of **recurrent childhood UTIs**; diagnosed by **MCU (micturating cystourethrogram)**; high grades scar the kidney.
- **Acute scrotal pain during play, sudden onset → testicular torsion**; Doppler shows absent flow, and exploration confirms a twisted (± gangrenous) testis. Torsion is a **6-hour** surgical emergency — when in doubt, explore.
- **Phimosis:** a 3-year-old with **ballooning of the foreskin** on micturition and a narrow tip; physiological phimosis is normal to ~3–4 years, treat only if symptomatic.

**Memory anchor.** *PUV → ablate the valves. Recurrent child UTI → think VUR (MCU). Sudden acute scrotum at play → torsion, explore fast. Ballooning foreskin = phimosis.*

## 8.5 Penile carcinoma: match the surgery to the T stage and the nodes

- **Verrucous carcinoma around the glans, no palpable inguinal nodes → partial penectomy** (a 2 cm clear margin preserving a functional stump is adequate for this low-grade, locally invasive tumour).
- **T3 penile carcinoma with no palpable nodes → penectomy + inguinal node dissection** (the risk of micrometastasis in higher T stage justifies nodal clearance even with clinically negative groins).
- The lesson: penile-cancer surgery scales with **T stage and nodal status** — small/low-grade spares tissue, higher-stage adds nodal dissection.

**Memory anchor.** *Verrucous, node-negative → partial penectomy. Higher T stage → add inguinal node dissection.*

**Chapter 8 recap.** Blood at the meatus forbids the Foley and demands a RUG; low-risk elderly prostate cancer is watched, not cut, and spreads osteoblastically up Batson's plexus; small RCCs are spared nephrons; sterile pyuria unmasks TB; and penile-cancer surgery scales with stage.

# Chapter 9 — Head, Neck, and Neck Masses

## 9.1 Locate the neck lump by how it moves and where it sits

The examiner tests neck masses through **movement and position** — learn the discriminators:

- **Moves with swallowing AND with tongue protrusion, midline** → **thyroglossal cyst** (tethered to the hyoid/foramen caecum by the tract). Tongue protrusion is the specific clue.
- **Moves with swallowing only, midline/paramedian** → thyroid swelling.
- **Fluctuant swelling at the junction of upper and middle thirds of sternomastoid (upper anterior triangle), deep to the muscle** → **branchial cyst** (2nd cleft); presents in young adults.
- **Pulsatile mass at the angle of the mandible / carotid bifurcation, slowly growing, splaying the carotids ("Lyre's sign"), mobile side-to-side but not up-down (Fontaine's sign)** → **carotid body tumour (paraganglioma)**.

**Memory anchor.** *Tongue-protrusion movement = thyroglossal. Upper-third-of-SCM cyst = branchial. Pulsatile + Lyre's sign at the mandible angle = carotid body tumour.*

## 9.2 Oral cancer, flaps, and the drivers of adjuvant therapy

After radical surgery for oral cancer with **PMMC (pectoralis major myocutaneous) flap** reconstruction, the strongest indicator for **adjuvant radiotherapy** is **extranodal (extracapsular) extension** — tumour breaching the lymph-node capsule is the single most powerful adverse feature (positive margins are the other). Multiple nodes and T3 matter, but ENE outranks them.

**Memory anchor.** *Extranodal extension is the loudest call for adjuvant RT after oral-cancer surgery.*

## 9.3 The BCC on the face

A small **nodule on the nasolabial fold / face** in an older patient, excised and benign-behaving locally, is most often a **basal cell carcinoma** — the commonest skin cancer, sun-exposed sites, **locally invasive but rarely metastasising** (rodent ulcer with a rolled pearly edge). It outranks melanoma and SCC as the default facial-nodule answer.

**Memory anchor.** *Slow facial nodule, rolled pearly edge = BCC (rodent ulcer) — local menace, rare to spread.*

**Chapter 9 recap.** Neck lumps declare themselves by movement (tongue protrusion = thyroglossal), by position (upper-SCM = branchial), and by pulsatility (Lyre's sign = carotid body). Extranodal extension drives adjuvant radiotherapy, and the everyday facial nodule is a BCC until proven otherwise.

# Chapter 10 — Skin, Soft Tissue, Burns, and Reconstruction

## 10.1 How a skin graft survives — the three-day story

**The pattern.** "How does the graft get nutrition on day 3?" — and its companions on graft type.

**The concept.** A skin graft lives through three sequential phases:

1. **Imbibition (days 0–2):** the graft soaks up plasma from the bed by diffusion — it is *not yet* vascularised. (So on "day 3," early on, the answer keyed is **imbibition**; it is the phase the graft has just been surviving on.)
2. **Inosculation (days 2–4):** cut vessel ends in the graft kiss those in the bed.
3. **Neovascularisation (day 4 onward):** new vessels grow in.

**Split vs full thickness.** For a large **post-burn** raw area you use a **split-thickness skin graft (STSG)** — it takes more reliably on imperfect beds, covers large areas, and the donor site re-epithelialises. Full-thickness grafts are for small cosmetically critical sites.

**Memory anchor.** *Graft nutrition: Imbibition → Inosculation → Neovascularisation. Big burns get split-thickness grafts.*

## 10.2 Burns: debride, escharotomy, and graft — three different knives

Do not confuse the burn procedures:

- **Escharotomy** = incising a **circumferential full-thickness eschar** to relieve constriction (limb compartment syndrome, or chest wall restricting ventilation). It is a *release*, done through dead tissue.
- **Surgical debridement** = excising **necrotic tissue** from a deep partial-/full-thickness burn to prepare the bed **before grafting**.
- **Grafting** comes after a clean bed.

The photo of a limb with **linear release incisions along a circumferential burn** = escharotomy; **excision of sloughy necrotic tissue** = debridement.

**Memory anchor.** *Escharotomy = release a tight circumferential eschar. Debridement = cut away dead tissue before grafting.*

## 10.3 Scars, chronic wounds, and negative pressure

- **Keloid:** a scar that **extends beyond the original wound margins**, rich in **growth factors (TGF-β)** and collagen; **simple excision recurs** — so the true statement is "it contains growth factor," and the false one is "extended excision is the treatment of choice." Contrast the **hypertrophic scar**, which stays within the wound boundary.
- **Pressure sore staging:** **1** = non-blanching erythema (intact skin); **2** = partial-thickness loss; **3** = full-thickness into subcutaneous fat but **fascia intact**; **4** = through fascia into **muscle/bone/tendon**. So "full-thickness loss with muscle/bone destruction" = **Stage 4**.
- **VAC (vacuum-assisted closure)** works at **−125 mmHg** continuous negative pressure — it removes exudate, reduces oedema, and stimulates granulation. (Two separate pool items both key **−125 mmHg**.)

**Memory anchor.** *Keloid overflows the wound (TGF-β), recurs after excision. Pressure sore Stage 4 = into muscle/bone. VAC = −125 mmHg.*

## 10.4 Necrotising soft-tissue infection: cut early, cut often

A rapidly spreading soft-tissue infection (image) that is **Meleney's synergistic gangrene / necrotising fasciitis** is caused by **β-haemolytic streptococcus (often polymicrobial)** and demands **repeated radical surgical debridement (every ~24 h until clean)** plus antibiotics; **hyperbaric oxygen does have a role** (adjunct). So "hyperbaric oxygen has NO role" is the **false** statement. The governing principle: **source control by the knife, urgently and repeatedly** — antibiotics alone lose.

**Memory anchor.** *Necrotising fasciitis = strep, serial debridement, HBO helps. The knife saves, not antibiotics alone.*

## 10.5 Cleft lip and the flap of choice

**Cleft lip** repair follows the **"rule of 10s"** (≈10 weeks, 10 lb, Hb 10) — so the earliest practical correction is around **~3 months and up to 5–6 months** (the pool keys 5–6 months as the earliest age it accepts; cleft palate is repaired later, ~9–18 months). The **DIEP flap** (deep inferior epigastric perforator) — skin and fat with **no muscle sacrificed**, based on perforators of the deep inferior epigastric artery — is a favourite reconstructive image because it spares the rectus (less donor morbidity than TRAM).

**Memory anchor.** *Cleft lip ≈ rule of 10s (~3–6 months). DIEP flap = skin+fat, spares rectus muscle.*

**Chapter 10 recap.** Grafts survive by imbibition then inosculation then neovascularisation; burns need the right knife (escharotomy releases, debridement clears, then graft); keloids overflow and recur; pressure sores stage by depth; VAC runs at −125 mmHg; and necrotising infection is cured by repeated surgery, not antibiotics alone.

# Chapter 11 — Neurosurgical Trauma and Lesions

## 11.1 Read the haemorrhage by its shape and story

- **Saccular (berry) aneurysm** of the circle of Willis ruptures into the **subarachnoid space → subarachnoid haemorrhage** (thunderclap headache). This is the pool's most-repeated neuro fact.
- **Subdural haemorrhage:** an **alcoholic/elderly** patient, **trauma a week ago**, now confused/ataxic — a **crescent-shaped** collection from torn **bridging veins**; classically **crosses suture lines** and evolves slowly.
- **Extradural (epidural) haemorrhage:** trauma → lucid interval → deterioration; **biconvex/lens-shaped**, arterial (middle meningeal), **does not cross sutures**.

**GCS in one breath.** Eyes (4), Verbal (5), Motor (6), best score 15. Worked example from the pool: **confused speech (V4)** + **eyes open to pain (E2)** + **localises pain (M5)** = **11**.

**Memory anchor.** *Berry aneurysm → SAH. Old trauma + alcoholic + crescent = subdural (bridging veins, crosses sutures). Lens-shaped + lucid interval = extradural.*

## 11.2 Raised pressure, ulcers, and posterior-fossa tumours

- **Cushing's ulcer:** peptic ulceration from **head injury / raised intracranial pressure** (vagal drive to acid). (Pair once more with **Curling's = burns**.)
- **Medulloblastoma:** a posterior-fossa (cerebellar) **PNET** arising from **primitive/immature embryonal neuroectodermal cells** of the external granular layer — the childhood midline cerebellar tumour.

**Memory anchor.** *Cushing's ulcer = head injury/raised ICP. Medulloblastoma = embryonal cells, cerebellum, child.*

**Chapter 11 recap.** Bleeds are read by shape and story: berry → SAH, crescent + old trauma → subdural, lens + lucid interval → extradural. GCS is 4-5-6. Cushing's ulcer belongs to the head injury, and the child's midline cerebellar tumour is a medulloblastoma of embryonal cells.

# Chapter 12 — Trauma, Triage, and Surgical Critical Care

## 12.1 The one decision that governs abdominal trauma: stable or not?

**The pattern.** Abdominal trauma questions are really one question wearing different clothes: **is the patient haemodynamically stable?**

**The rule.**
- **Unstable** (hypotension, guarding) with penetrating or blunt abdominal trauma → **immediate exploratory laparotomy**. Do not waste time on CT. FAST/DPL only add delay when laparotomy is already indicated. *(Epigastric stab + hypotension + guarding → laparotomy.)*
- **Stable** blunt trauma → **CECT abdomen**, the gold standard for grading solid-organ (liver, spleen, kidney) injury. *(Stable child post-RTA with lumbar tenderness → CECT.)*
- **Anterior abdominal stab wound in a child, even if stable** → the pool favours **exploratory laparotomy** (high risk of hollow-viscus injury that imaging misses). *(Note the deliberate contrast: blunt-stable → image; penetrating → low threshold to explore.)*

FAST is the tool for the **unstable** patient (bedside, fast, detects free fluid); CT is for the **stable** patient (detail, grading).

**Memory anchor.** *Unstable → laparotomy now. Stable blunt → CECT. Penetrating (esp. child) → low threshold to explore.*

## 12.2 Chest trauma and the paradox of a flail segment

After an RTA with chest pain and dyspnoea, **paradoxical breathing** — a segment of chest wall that **sucks in on inspiration and bulges on expiration** — signals a **flail chest** (≥2 ribs fractured in ≥2 places, creating a free-floating segment). The underlying lung contusion, not the moving segment itself, drives the hypoxia.

**Memory anchor.** *Paradoxical chest movement after RTA = flail chest.*

## 12.3 Triage colours and sepsis scores

- **Triage (mass-casualty):** **Green = walking wounded / ambulatory** (minor, can wait); **Yellow** = delayed; **Red** = immediate life threat; **Black** = dead/expectant. So "green category" = **ambulatory patients**.
- **qSOFA** (rapid bedside sepsis risk, outside ICU): **Systolic BP ≤100, Respiratory rate ≥22, altered mentation (GCS <15)** — score ≥2 flags high risk. Note it is **SBP + RR + GCS**, no lab test required — that is the whole point of "quick" SOFA.

**Memory anchor.** *Green triage = walking wounded. qSOFA = low SBP + fast RR + altered GCS (no labs).*

## 12.4 The IVC hiding behind the duodenum

During duodenal surgery (mobilisation, Whipple's), **massive bleeding from a posterior vessel** most likely means the **inferior vena cava** — the **IVC lies directly behind the 2nd/3rd parts of the duodenum**. Anatomical adjacency is the whole answer: the duodenum is retroperitoneal and the great vein is its back wall.

**Memory anchor.** *Posterior-duodenal catastrophic bleed = IVC (it's the duodenum's back wall).*

## 12.5 Fluids and feeding in the surgical patient

- **Ringer's lactate** by composition: **Na 130, Cl 109, K 4, Ca ~1.5–3, lactate ~28 mmol/L** — the near-isotonic balanced crystalloid for resuscitation. Recognise it by the **lactate** and the **calcium**, which the alternatives (Isolyte, Haemaccel) lack in that pattern.
- **TPN in the septic ICU patient → hyperglycaemia** is the commonest metabolic complication (dextrose load + insulin resistance of sepsis). Watch glucose closely.
- **Variceal-risk upper-GI bleed:** an alcoholic with haematemesis and portal-vein/echobright-liver changes — after starting a PPI, the immediate priority in the keyed answer is **IV fluids and electrolyte resuscitation** (stabilise first); **endoscopy** to band varices follows once resuscitated. Resuscitation precedes diagnosis — the ABC principle applies even when varices are obvious.

**Memory anchor.** *Ringer's = balanced crystalloid (spot the lactate + calcium). TPN in sepsis → hyperglycaemia. GI bleed → resuscitate before you scope.*

## 12.6 Monitoring, transplantation, and the coagulation waveform

- **Thromboelastography (TEG):** a **viscoelastic** whole-blood assay drawing a waveform of clot **initiation → amplification → stabilisation → fibrinolysis**; it guides targeted intraoperative transfusion (which component to give) better than isolated lab values.
- **Maastricht classification** of donation after **circulatory** death: **Category III = awaiting cardiac arrest** (controlled DCD, withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment in a patient who has not yet arrested). (I = brought in dead, II = unsuccessful resuscitation, IV = arrest after brainstem death, V = in-hospital arrest.)

**Memory anchor.** *TEG = whole-blood clot waveform, guides transfusion. Maastricht III = awaiting (controlled) cardiac arrest.*

**Chapter 12 recap.** Every abdominal-trauma question resolves on stable-vs-unstable; a flail segment moves paradoxically; green triage walks; qSOFA needs no labs; the IVC is the duodenum's back wall; resuscitate before you scope; and TEG reads the clot as a living waveform.

# Chapter 13 — Orthopaedic Trauma and Limb Emergencies

*(Orthopaedics is a surgical discipline, and the pool folds in a large orthopaedic-trauma block. Here are the highest-yield, genuinely surgical patterns — fracture eponyms, dislocations, and the limb-threatening emergency.)*

## 13.1 The limb-threatening emergency outranks everything

**The intern's priority.** Among fracture patients waiting to be seen, the **first** to escalate is the one whose **finger is blackening** — that is **critical ischaemia / impending gangrene (vascular compromise)**, which threatens the limb within hours. An abrasion, an inability to extend, or an isolated intra-articular fracture can wait; **dead tissue cannot**. This is the triage instinct surgery keeps testing: *perfusion before position.*

**Compartment syndrome and fasciotomy.** Rising compartment pressure presents with **pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch**, and later the late "P"s; treat with **fasciotomy dividing all four layers** (skin → subcutaneous → superficial fascia → deep fascia). **Popliteal artery injury** classically accompanies **knee dislocation / proximal tibia trauma** — always check the pulse below a dislocated knee.

**Memory anchor.** *Blackening finger = vascular emergency, see first. Compartment syndrome → full four-layer fasciotomy. Dislocated knee → check the popliteal artery.*

## 13.2 Fracture-dislocation eponyms — the paired ones

Examiners love eponyms because they compress a whole injury into a name. Learn the *forearm pair* together:

- **Monteggia:** fracture of the **proximal ulna** + **dislocation of the radial head**. Memory: **"MUGR"** — **M**onteggia = **U**lna fracture, **G**aleazzi = **R**adius fracture.
- **Galeazzi:** fracture of the **distal radius** + **dislocation of the distal radio-ulnar joint**.
- **Colles':** **distal radius** fracture with **dorsal** angulation → **"dinner-fork" deformity** (fall on outstretched hand, older woman).
- **Chauffeur's fracture:** fracture of the **radial styloid**.
- **Cubitus valgus** develops late after a **lateral condyle fracture** (non-union/growth disturbance) → tardy ulnar nerve palsy.

**Memory anchor.** *MUGR: Monteggia=Ulna, Galeazzi=Radius. Colles = dinner fork (dorsal). Chauffeur = radial styloid. Lateral condyle → cubitus valgus.*

## 13.3 Dislocations and their signs

- **Luxatio erecta:** rare **inferior** shoulder dislocation — arm locked **overhead in hyperabduction**; risk to the axillary nerve.
- **Hamilton's ruler test** positive → **anterior shoulder dislocation** (a straight ruler touches acromion and lateral epicondyle because the rounded deltoid contour is lost).
- **Perilunate dislocation:** the **lunate stays aligned with the radius** while the **capitate dislocates dorsally** off it (in the pool's phrasing: radius–scaphoid–capitate line disrupted, lunate out of plane in the lunate variant) — a high-energy carpal injury easily missed.

**Memory anchor.** *Luxatio erecta = arm stuck up (inferior dislocation). Hamilton ruler = anterior shoulder dislocation.*

## 13.4 The paediatric bone and the tendon avulsions

- **Greenstick fracture:** an **incomplete** fracture unique to **children** — one cortex breaks, the other bends, because young bone is soft with thick periosteum. A **torus (buckle)** fracture buckles one cortex.
- **Jersey finger:** avulsion of **FDP** from the distal phalanx (finger forcibly extended while gripping — the rugby jersey). **Mallet finger** is the extensor counterpart (DIP droop).
- **Scaphoid:** the bone that most commonly fractures in a **fall on the outstretched hand** — beware the normal early X-ray and the risk of avascular necrosis (retrograde blood supply).

**Memory anchor.** *Greenstick = child's bending fracture. Jersey finger = FDP avulsion. FOOSH → scaphoid (watch for AVN).*

**Chapter 13 recap.** Perfusion beats position — a blackening finger is seen first, a dislocated knee gets its popliteal pulse checked, and compartments get a full four-layer release. Eponyms pair up (MUGR), the dinner fork is a Colles', and greenstick fractures belong to children.

# Chapter 14 — The Surgical Toolkit: Sutures, Knots, Drains, and Instruments

## 14.1 Know your suture by material and behaviour

- **Catgut** (the "made of submucosa" clue): a **natural absorbable** suture from **animal (sheep/cattle) submucosa**, **degraded by enzymatic (proteolytic) digestion** — so "degraded by enzymatic degradation" is the true statement, and "not degraded" is false.
- **Polydioxanone (PDS):** **synthetic, absorbable, monofilament** — retains ~50% strength at 4 weeks, fully absorbed by ~180–210 days (by **hydrolysis**, not enzymes). Key facts: **monofilament** (not braided), **not collagen-derived**, **not non-absorbable**.
- The absorbable/non-absorbable and monofilament/braided axes are what examiners cross-question — braided sutures (silk, Vicryl) handle well but harbour bacteria; monofilaments (PDS, Prolene) resist infection but handle stiffly.

**Memory anchor.** *Catgut = animal submucosa, enzymatic breakdown. PDS = synthetic monofilament, hydrolysed, 50% strength at 4 weeks.*

## 14.2 Knots, drains, and access instruments

- **Surgeon's knot:** a **double throw on the first tie** so it grips and won't slip while you lay the second throw — used where tissue is under tension.
- **Closed suction drain** (e.g. Redivac): a compressible reservoir generating **negative pressure**; distinguish from a **Penrose** (open, passive, capillary) and an **underwater-seal** (chest) drain.
- **Suprapubic catheter insertion / cystoscopy** uses a **cysto-urethroscope** (the scope that navigates bladder and urethra). Not a nephroscope (kidney) or ureteroscope (ureter).
- **Intraosseous cannula:** when IV access fails in resuscitation, drive a needle into the **anteromedial proximal tibia (1–2 cm below and medial to the tibial tuberosity)** — marrow is a **non-collapsible venous channel**, so drug onset nearly matches IV.

**Memory anchor.** *Surgeon's knot = double first throw (anti-slip). Closed suction drain = negative pressure. Cysto-urethroscope for suprapubic access. IO line → proximal tibia when IV fails.*

**Chapter 14 recap.** Sutures sort on two axes (absorbable vs not, mono vs braided); catgut is enzyme-eaten submucosa, PDS is hydrolysed monofilament; the surgeon's knot resists slipping; drains differ by mechanism; and the failed drip becomes an intraosseous line in the proximal tibia.

## Editorial Note — What Was Left Out, and Why

This book is built from the "Surgery" pool, but that pool (as flagged in the repository's own notes) has absorbed a number of **misclassified questions** from other subjects during automated sorting. To keep this a *surgery* book with an honest signal-to-noise ratio, the following strays were deliberately **not** taught here, because they belong elsewhere:

- **Paediatric developmental milestones** (social smile, weight doubling, vocabulary, teeth, head circumference, grasp) → Paediatrics.
- **Obstetrics & Gynaecology** (quickening, breech types, shoulder-dystocia manoeuvres, occipito-anterior position, cervical dilatation rate, neoadjuvant chemo for cervical cancer) → OBG.
- **Pure medicine/pharmacology** (amiodarone pulmonary fibrosis, clonidine rebound hypertension, fasting hypoglycaemia causes, HIE, TAPVC, ARPKD genetics beyond the surgical angle, JE vaccine) → Medicine/Paediatrics/PSM.
- **ENT audiology** (Rinne air-bone gap) → ENT.
- **Bone physiology and rheumatology** not tied to a surgical decision (RA cell type, costal cartilage joint type, most vascular zone of bone, trigger finger joint, De Quervain's muscles, Thomas/Trendelenburg tests, Tinel's sign, POP chemistry, bone-tumour trivia) → Orthopaedics/Anatomy. The genuinely *surgical* orthopaedic trauma (Chapter 13) was retained.

A couple of individual answer keys in the source pool look internally inconsistent (e.g. the high-AFP alcoholic keyed as "alcoholic hepatitis," which the clinical numbers argue is HCC; and the oesophageal-perforation contrast keyed as barium where water-soluble contrast is the safer first choice). Where that happened, this book **teaches the sound clinical principle** and flags the discrepancy in the text, rather than repeating a doubtful key. If you are the repository owner, those items are worth reviewing against a current standard reference.
