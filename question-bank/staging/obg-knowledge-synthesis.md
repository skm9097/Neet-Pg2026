## Obstetrics — Labour Complications

### Q106 — Shoulder dystocia management

A primigravida delivers the fetal head but progress halts. The "Turtle sign" is noted. The obstetrician flexes the maternal thighs sharply onto the abdomen. Which manoeuvre is being performed?

- A. Zavanelli manoeuvre
- B. McRoberts manoeuvre
- C. Rubin II manoeuvre
- D. Woods screw manoeuvre

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. McRoberts manoeuvre** — Hyperflexion of maternal thighs onto the abdomen flattens lumbar lordosis, rotates symphysis pubis superiorly, and increases the functional AP diameter of the pelvis; it is the FIRST step in shoulder dystocia management.

</details>

### Q107 — Shoulder dystocia: last resort

After McRoberts, suprapubic pressure, Rubin II, and Woods screw manoeuvres all fail for shoulder dystocia, the obstetrician decides to push the fetal head back into the uterus and proceed to caesarean section. This is:

- A. Mauriceau-Smellie-Veit manoeuvre
- B. Prague manoeuvre
- C. Zavanelli (cephalic replacement) manoeuvre
- D. Ritgen manoeuvre

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Zavanelli (cephalic replacement) manoeuvre** — Involves rotating the head to occiput anterior, then manually pushing it back into the vagina followed by emergency caesarean section; used as a last resort when all other manoeuvres fail.

</details>

### Q108 — Cord prolapse: immediate management

A G2P1 at 38 weeks presents with rupture of membranes and the umbilical cord is visible at the introitus with fetal bradycardia on CTG. What is the MOST important immediate step?

- A. Urgent caesarean section
- B. Manual replacement of cord into the uterus
- C. Digital elevation of presenting part to relieve cord compression
- D. Administer tocolytic immediately

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Digital elevation of presenting part to relieve cord compression** — Immediate relief of cord compression by manually pushing the presenting part upward is the first priority; this buys time while preparing for emergency caesarean section (definitive management).

### Q109 — Uterine inversion classification

A woman who has just delivered is brought in with haemorrhagic shock. On examination, the fundus is not palpable abdominally and a dark mass is seen protruding through the introitus. This corresponds to which degree of uterine inversion?

- A. First degree (incomplete)
- B. Second degree (complete)
- C. Third degree (prolapsed)
- D. Fourth degree (total)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Third degree (prolapsed)** — Third-degree inversion: the inverted fundus passes through the cervix and protrudes beyond the introitus. Fourth degree involves the vagina also inverting; first degree stays within the uterine cavity; second degree reaches the cervical os.

</details>

### Q110 — Uterine inversion management

Acute puerperal uterine inversion with haemodynamic instability is recognised. After resuscitation, manual repositioning fails. Which pharmacological agent should be given to relax the uterus BEFORE attempting Johnson's hydrostatic method?

- A. Oxytocin
- B. Ergometrine
- C. Terbutaline (tocolytic)
- D. Carboprost

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Terbutaline (tocolytic)** — Uterine relaxation (tocolysis with terbutaline, nitroglycerin, or MgSO4) is required to allow repositioning; uterotonic agents (oxytocin, ergometrine, carboprost) cause uterine contraction and must NOT be given until after the uterus is successfully replaced.

</details>

### Q111 — Instrumental delivery: vacuum vs forceps

A primigravida has been pushing for 2 hours. Fetal head is at +2 station, occiput anterior, with caput and moulding +1. CTG shows late decelerations. Cervix is fully dilated. Which statement about vacuum extraction (Ventouse) versus forceps is CORRECT?

- A. Forceps is preferred when fetal scalp condition must be protected (preterm < 34 weeks)
- B. Vacuum can be used for face presentation
- C. Forceps requires less maternal effort than vacuum
- D. Vacuum is associated with higher rates of maternal perineal trauma than forceps

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A. Forceps is preferred when fetal scalp condition must be protected (preterm < 34 weeks)** — Vacuum is contraindicated below 34 weeks (risk of cephalhaematoma and intracranial haemorrhage in preterm neonates); forceps is preferred. Vacuum is contraindicated for face presentation; forceps causes more maternal perineal trauma, not less.

</details>

### Q112 — Forceps: absolute contraindication

Which of the following is an ABSOLUTE contraindication to the use of outlet forceps?

- A. Cervix fully dilated
- B. Unengaged fetal head
- C. Ruptured membranes
- D. Occiput posterior position

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. Unengaged fetal head** — Forceps (and vacuum) must never be applied to an unengaged head; engagement (head at or below the ischial spines, i.e. ≥ 0 station) is an absolute prerequisite for any instrumental delivery.

</details>

## Obstetrics — Caesarean Section

### Q113 — Classical vs LSCS

A woman at 28 weeks gestation has a transverse lie with back-down presentation requiring emergency caesarean. Which uterine incision is preferred?

- A. Lower uterine segment transverse (Kerr)
- B. Lower uterine segment vertical (Kronig)
- C. Classical (upper segment vertical)
- D. De Lee incision

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Classical (upper segment vertical)** — Classical caesarean is indicated when the lower segment is not well formed (extreme prematurity < 28 weeks), transverse lie with back-down (inaccessible lower segment), and anterior placenta praevia with extensive vasculature. It carries higher risk of uterine rupture in future pregnancies.

</details>

### Q115 — Amniotic fluid embolism

A 32-year-old multiparous woman suddenly develops severe respiratory distress, hypotension, and cyanosis within minutes of delivery. She rapidly develops DIC and cardiac arrest. The MOST likely diagnosis is:

- A. Pulmonary thromboembolism
- B. Amniotic fluid embolism (AFE)
- C. Eclampsia
- D. Aortic dissection

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. Amniotic fluid embolism (AFE)** — AFE classically presents as sudden cardiovascular collapse + respiratory distress + DIC in the peripartum period; the triad of hypoxia, hypotension, and coagulopathy during or immediately after labour is pathognomonic. Mortality > 80%.

</details>

### Q116 — Uterine tamponade balloon

After a lower segment caesarean for placenta praevia, bleeding from the lower segment continues despite oxytocin, ergometrine, and carboprost. The surgeon inserts a Bakri balloon into the uterus. What is the PRINCIPLE of action?

- B. It applies direct intrauterine tamponade pressure exceeding arterial perfusion pressure

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. </details>

### Q117 — Face presentation: management

A primigravida at term in active labour is found to have a face presentation with the chin (mentum) directed posteriorly (mento-posterior). What is the recommended management?

- B. Manual rotation to mento-anterior followed by forceps

### Q118 — Brow presentation

A term primigravida is in active labour. On vaginal examination, the examiner palpates the orbital ridges, nose, and anterior fontanelle without feeling the mouth or chin. The station is −1. This presentation is:

- A. Face presentation
- B. Brow presentation

## Obstetrics — Twin Pregnancy

### Q119 — Twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome (TTTS)

A monochorionic diamniotic twin pregnancy at 22 weeks is found on ultrasound to have one sac with polyhydramnios (DVP > 8 cm) and the other sac with oligohydramnios (DVP < 2 cm). The smaller twin appears "stuck." What is the FIRST-LINE treatment for stage II–III TTTS?

- A. Selective fetal reduction
- B. Serial amnioreduction
- C. Fetoscopic laser photocoagulation of anastomotic vessels
- D. Delivery at 28 weeks

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Fetoscopic laser photocoagulation of anastomotic vessels** — Fetoscopic laser ablation of placental vascular anastomoses is the first-line treatment for stage II–IV TTTS before 26 weeks; it addresses the underlying pathophysiology (unequal blood exchange via arteriovenous anastomoses) and improves dual survival compared to amnioreduction.

</details>

### Q120 — Monoamniotic twins: specific risk

Which complication is unique to monoamniotic (MA) twins and is responsible for their high perinatal mortality?

- A. Twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome
- B. Cord entanglement
- C. Selective intrauterine growth restriction
- D. Discordant anomalies

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. Cord entanglement** — Monoamniotic twins share a single amniotic sac, allowing the umbilical cords to intertwine (cord entanglement/knotting), which can cause sudden fetal death. TTTS requires a monochorionic placenta but can occur in diamniotic twins; cord entanglement is exclusive to MA twins.

</details>

### Q121 — Twin anaemia polycythaemia sequence (TAPS)

Monochorionic twin pregnancy: one twin has MCA-PSV > 1.5 MoM (anaemic) and the other has MCA-PSV < 1.0 MoM (polycythaemic) without the sac volume discordance seen in TTTS. This describes:

- A. Twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome (TTTS)
- B. Twin anaemia polycythaemia sequence (TAPS)
- C. Selective IUGR
- D. Discordant growth

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. Twin anaemia polycythaemia sequence (TAPS)** — TAPS is characterised by large inter-twin Hb differences via slow transfusion through tiny placental anastomoses, without the amniotic fluid discordance of TTTS; MCA-PSV Doppler is the diagnostic tool (elevated in anaemic twin, low in polycythaemic twin).

</details>

## Obstetrics — IUGR and Doppler

### Q122 — IUGR: absent end-diastolic flow

A growth-restricted fetus at 30 weeks has absent end-diastolic flow (AEDF) on umbilical artery Doppler. Which additional Doppler finding would indicate imminent fetal compromise requiring immediate delivery?

- A. MCA pulsatility index < 5th centile
- B. Reversed end-diastolic flow (REDF) in umbilical artery
- C. DV pulsatility index > 95th centile
- D. Reversed a-wave in ductus venosus (DV)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D. Reversed a-wave in ductus venosus (DV)** — Reversed a-wave in the ductus venosus (indicating raised central venous pressure and right heart failure) is the most ominous Doppler finding in IUGR and is an indication for immediate delivery regardless of gestational age; it reflects terminal cardiovascular decompensation.

</details>

### Q123 — Brain-sparing in IUGR

In a fetus with suspected uteroplacental insufficiency, which Doppler ratio is calculated to assess "brain-sparing" (preferential redistribution of blood flow to the brain)?

- A. Umbilical artery PI / Uterine artery PI
- B. MCA PI / Umbilical artery PI (cerebroplacental ratio, CPR)
- C. Ductus venosus PI / MCA PI
- D. Aortic isthmus PI / MCA PI

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. MCA PI / Umbilical artery PI (cerebroplacental ratio, CPR)** — A CPR < 1.08 (or MCA-PI below 5th centile with elevated UA-PI) indicates brain-sparing: vasodilation of cerebral vessels to maintain oxygen delivery to the brain in the context of placental insufficiency.

</details>

## Obstetrics — Preterm Labour and Tocolytics

A 28-year-old primigravida at 30 weeks presents with regular uterine contractions and cervical dilation of 2 cm. She has no contraindications to tocolysis. Cervical length on transvaginal ultrasound is 18 mm. Which tocolytic agent is currently considered FIRST-LINE by WHO and RCOG guidelines?

- C. Nifedipine (calcium channel blocker)
- D. Atosiban (oxytocin antagonist)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. ### Q125 — Atosiban mechanism

Atosiban is used as a tocolytic in preterm labour. What is its mechanism of action?

- A. Beta-2 adrenergic receptor agonist → myometrial relaxation
- B. Competitive oxytocin/vasopressin receptor antagonist → reduced myometrial contractions
- C. Inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis via COX-2
- D. L-type calcium channel blockade in myometrium

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. Competitive oxytocin/vasopressin receptor antagonist → reduced myometrial contractions** — Atosiban competitively blocks oxytocin and vasopressin V1a receptors in the myometrium and decidua, reducing uterine contractility; it has fewer systemic side effects than ritodrine and is preferred in Europe.

</details>

### Q131 — Cardiac disease in pregnancy: absolute contraindication

Which cardiac condition is an ABSOLUTE contraindication to pregnancy due to > 50% maternal mortality?

### Q132 — LMWH in pregnancy

A pregnant woman at 12 weeks with a previous deep vein thrombosis requires thromboprophylaxis. She is started on low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH). What is the advantage of LMWH over unfractionated heparin (UFH) in pregnancy?

## Gynaecology — Menopause and HRT

### Q133 — Menopause: vasomotor symptoms

A 51-year-old woman presents with frequent hot flushes and night sweats for 8 months following her last menstrual period. FSH is 60 IU/L. She has no contraindications. What is the MOST effective treatment for her vasomotor symptoms?

- A. Low-dose SSRIs (venlafaxine)
- B. Tibolone
- C. Combined oestrogen-progestogen HRT (systemic)
- D. Black cohosh supplements

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Combined oestrogen-progestogen HRT (systemic)** — Systemic HRT (oestrogen + progestogen for women with a uterus) is the most effective treatment for menopausal vasomotor symptoms; all other options (SSRIs, tibolone, phytoestrogens) are second-line alternatives for women who cannot take HRT.

</details>

### Q134 — HRT and endometrial protection

A 53-year-old woman with an intact uterus requires HRT for severe menopausal symptoms. Why must progestogen be added to oestrogen therapy?

- A. To prevent cardiovascular events
- B. To protect against oestrogen-induced endometrial hyperplasia and cancer
- C. To prevent breast cancer from oestrogen
- D. To improve mood and libido

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Women after hysterectomy can take oestrogen-only HRT.

</details>

### Q136 — AUB-O (ovulatory dysfunction)

A 35-year-old woman presents with irregular, heavy, unpredictable periods. She is obese with BMI 34. Pelvic ultrasound is normal, endometrial biopsy shows simple hyperplasia without atypia, and serum progesterone in luteal phase is < 3 ng/mL. The underlying mechanism of her AUB is most likely:

## Gynaecology — Chromosomal and Developmental Disorders

### Q139 — Turner syndrome

A 16-year-old girl presents with primary amenorrhoea, short stature, and webbed neck. Karyotype is 45,X0. Which investigation would show streak gonads on imaging?

- A. MRI of pituitary
- B. Pelvic ultrasound

### Q140 — Rokitansky (MRKH) syndrome

A 17-year-old presents with primary amenorrhoea, normal secondary sexual characteristics, and a blind vaginal pouch of 2 cm. Ultrasound shows absent uterus with bilateral normal ovaries. Karyotype is 46,XX. This is:

- A. Complete androgen insensitivity syndrome (CAIS)

### Q141 — Androgen insensitivity syndrome (AIS)

A phenotypically female 18-year-old presents with primary amenorrhoea and absence of pubic/axillary hair. Karyotype is 46,XY. Testes are found in the inguinal canals. What is the MOST IMPORTANT long-term management concern?

- A. Cyclical oestrogen-progestogen HRT to induce menstruation
- B. Gonadectomy to prevent malignant transformation of intra-abdominal/inguinal testes

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. Gonadectomy to prevent malignant transformation of intra-abdominal/inguinal testes** — In complete AIS (46,XY), the testes have a 2–5% lifetime risk of malignant transformation (gonadoblastoma, dysgerminoma); gonadectomy is recommended after puberty (allowing puberty to complete with endogenous oestrogen from testosterone aromatisation), followed by oestrogen-only HRT.

</details>

## Gynaecology — Benign Conditions

### Q142 — Primary dysmenorrhoea

A 19-year-old nulliparous woman presents with severe, crampy lower abdominal pain starting 2–4 hours before menses and lasting 48–72 hours, relieved by NSAIDs. Pelvic examination is normal. The PATHOPHYSIOLOGICAL mechanism is:

- A. Elevated serum oestradiol causing myometrial spasm
- B. Excess prostaglandin F2α causing myometrial ischaemia
- C. Retrograde menstruation causing peritoneal inflammation
- D. Elevated prolactin causing luteal phase deficiency

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. Excess prostaglandin F2α causing myometrial ischaemia** — Primary dysmenorrhoea is caused by excessive prostaglandin F2α (PGF2α) production in the secretory endometrium, causing intense myometrial contractions → uterine ischaemia and pain; NSAIDs (COX inhibitors) reduce PGF2α synthesis and are first-line treatment.

</details>

**B. Marsupialization** — Marsupialization (surgical creation of a new duct opening to allow drainage) is the preferred definitive treatment for recurrent or large Bartholin's cysts; I&D alone has a high recurrence rate; Word catheter is good for acute abscesses; excision is reserved for older women (risk of haematoma, dyspareunia).

</details>

### Q146 — Stress urinary incontinence: mechanism

A 45-year-old multiparous woman leaks urine on coughing, sneezing, and exercise. She denies urgency or frequency. Urodynamics shows involuntary urine loss with increased intra-abdominal pressure without detrusor contraction. The pathophysiological mechanism is:

### Q148 — IVF: ovarian hyperstimulation (OHSS)

A 29-year-old woman with PCOS undergoing IVF develops severe abdominal distension, nausea, and shortness of breath 5 days after oocyte retrieval. Ultrasound shows bilateral enlarged ovaries (each > 10 cm) with massive ascites. Haematocrit is 52%. This complication is:

## Obstetrics — Antepartum Haemorrhage

### Q151 — APH: Vasa praevia distinction (clinical scenario)

A primigravida at 38 weeks presents with sudden onset painless bright red vaginal bleeding immediately after spontaneous rupture of membranes. Fetal bradycardia (FHR 80 bpm) develops within minutes. What diagnosis must be IMMEDIATELY excluded?

- A. Placenta praevia
- B. Placental abruption
- C. Vasa praevia with fetal vessel rupture
- D. Cervical ectropion

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C. Vasa praevia with fetal vessel rupture** — The hallmark of vasa praevia rupture is painless bright red bleeding at the time of membrane rupture + immediate fetal distress; bleeding is FETAL blood (not maternal), hence rapid fetal exsanguination and death without immediate caesarean. ### Q153 — Levator ani anatomy

Which muscles form the levator ani (the main component of the pelvic diaphragm)?

### Q155 — DUB: tranexamic acid

A 38-year-old woman with heavy regular periods (changing pads every 2 hours) and normal pelvic ultrasound and endometrial biopsy (AUB-E) does not wish to use hormonal treatment. What is the MOST APPROPRIATE non-hormonal first-line treatment?

- A. Norethisterone 5 mg three times daily cyclically
- B. Tranexamic acid 1 g three times daily during menstruation
- C. Danazol 200 mg daily
- D. GnRH agonist monthly injection

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B. Tranexamic acid 1 g three times daily during menstruation** — Tranexamic acid (antifibrinolytic) reduces menstrual blood loss by 40–50% and is the first-line non-hormonal pharmacological treatment for heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB); NSAIDs (mefenamic acid) are an alternative non-hormonal option. Hormonal options (LNG-IUS, OCPs) are preferred if contraception is also required.

</details>
